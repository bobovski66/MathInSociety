Which requests should be easiest to reach?

A community center can shorten some callers' routes through its phone menu. But which callers should get the shortest route, and how will we judge whether a design works well?

The problem: four services, twenty calls

A neighborhood community center wants callers to reach information about bus schedules, food pantry hours, clinic appointments, or shelter. A caller answers one yes-or-no question at each step; we record the choices as 0 or 1. The resulting code describes the route through the menu.

The center has recorded the following requests:

ServiceNumber of calls
Bus schedules10
Food pantry hours5
Clinic appointments3
Shelter information2

You have four stopping codes available. Use every code exactly once, giving each service its own route:

Available stopping codeQuestions needed to reach it
01
102
1103
1113

Notice the tradeoff: assigning a shorter route to one service leaves longer routes for others. You will recommend an assignment, calculate how many questions callers answer altogether, and consider whether that total captures what matters most to the community.

Your initial post

Work through the five parts below in order. You don't need to find every possible arrangement or prove that yours is the only good one. You do need to make a definite recommendation, support it with calculations, and explain your reasoning.

  1. Choose a menu. Assign 0, 10, 110, and 111 to the four services, using each exactly once. Explain why you gave your shortest code to that particular service. Are you prioritizing the number of callers, the urgency of a request, or something else?
  2. Calculate the workload. For each service, multiply its number of calls by the number of questions in the code you assigned. Show all four products and add them. Then calculate the total for a different menu that asks every caller exactly two questions. Which menu asks fewer questions overall, and by how many?
  3. Change the notation, not the quantity. Take the total number of questions for your proposed menu and write that same number in binary (base 2) and octal (base 8). Show how you found each representation: use place values or repeated division. You can check the octal answer by grouping your binary digits in threes, starting from the right. Explain briefly why both numerals describe the same workload.
  4. Examine the tradeoff. Imagine that someone requesting shelter information needs help immediately. Does that change your recommendation? If you would change the codes, say which services you would switch and what would happen to the total number of questions. If you would keep your design, explain why. What would you want to know about actual callers before making a final decision?
  5. Connect it to experience. Describe a phone menu, online form, or similar sequence of choices you've used. What made it easy or frustrating? Identify one important feature of that experience that our simple count of questions does not measure.

Reply to a classmate

Choose a post that makes a different code assignment or gives a different reason for its choice. Check one row of the workload calculation and one base conversion, explaining what you checked. Then compare the two designs: who benefits from each one, and what question would you want answered before recommending one for actual use?

It's fine to disagree. Focus on the numbers and the needs of callers, rather than simply saying that one arrangement is better.
