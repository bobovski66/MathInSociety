Price, demand, and a defensible recommendation.

The task / A clear mathematical comparison

Choose a price, and say what the comparison assumes

A student club runs a one-day T-shirt booth. Setup, signs, and the booth rental cost $300. Each shirt costs $6 to buy and package. The club is considering prices of $12, $14, and $16. In the first comparison, assume every shirt prepared is sold, and compare all three prices at exactly 60 sales.

Work the problem before reading classmates’ posts. A table, labeled calculation, graph, or photo of organized handwritten work can communicate the mathematics. Use your class discussion board to post; the drafting fields here only save in this browser.

Part A / Hold the sales quantity fixed

Build all three pricing models

Define q as shirts prepared and sold during this event. The cost function is the same for every price, but each price changes revenue and the profit margin. Make a comparison table with the following columns:

  1. Write C(q), R(q), and P(q) for each price, with units and a defined q.
  2. Find each exact break-even threshold. When it is fractional, also give the smallest whole count avoiding a loss.
  3. Calculate profit at 60 sales and identify the largest value under this fixed-sales assumption.
  4. Compare the three margins and explain one entry in your table in words.
Part B / Add an explicit demand scenario

What changes when different prices attract different sales volumes?

Now suppose a separate planning estimate predicts these sales at the three prices. Treat the estimates as hypothetical inputs, not observed facts. Assume the club prepares exactly the number it sells in each scenario.

A useful distinction

Part A asks what follows if sales quantities are equal. Part B asks what follows if these demand estimates apply. Neither calculation alone proves how real buyers will respond. Name an observation that would help test the assumption behind your recommendation.

Respond to a classmate’s mathematics

Check one calculation or compare an assumption. You can use a classmate’s same recommendation and investigate why their reasoning works; classmates do not need to choose different prices.

For a concrete follow-up, keep F=$300 and v=$6. Suppose the $16 plan sells 45 shirts. How many whole shirts would the $14 plan need to sell to earn strictly more profit? Show the comparison inequality and check the neighboring whole counts. Explain what this threshold does and does not establish about demand.

Review before posting

  • My quantities and dollar outputs have units and a time period.
  • I distinguished exact break-even from a rounded whole-item threshold.
  • I calculated each scenario using its own price and sales quantity.
  • My recommendation states its assumptions and an evidence question.
  • My reply contributes a calculation or a specific mathematical observation.
