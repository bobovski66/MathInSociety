Module 9 Discussion — Same Constraints, Different Objectives

Optimize one feasible region from three different decision-making perspectives.

How this discussion works

Solve, present, then respond

Work the problem below before reading classmates’ solutions. In your initial post, show enough mathematical work that another student can follow your method, then state the result in the language of the situation. Post your solution in the class discussion board; this page itself does not submit responses.

A clear table, calculation, labeled graph, or photo of neatly organized handwritten work is fine when appropriate. The goal is mathematical communication, not extra prose.

Context

The problem

The school print room from Module 8 has the same production limits: posters are represented by \(x\), banners by \(y\), and the feasible plans satisfy

\[x+2y\le24,\qquad 2x+y\le30,\qquad x\ge0,\qquad y\ge0.\]

The physical constraints have not changed, but different people evaluating the event care about different outcomes. Choose one of the following simplified objective functions:

Objective A — Fundraising contribution
Maximize \(P=40x+55y\)
Objective B — Visibility score
Maximize \(S=30x+60y\)
Objective C — Sponsor-priority score
Maximize \(R=25x+70y\)

The numerical weights are model assumptions: they represent how much value that decision maker assigns to one poster or one banner. Your task is to optimize the chosen objective over the same feasible region and explain why changing the goal can change the preferred production plan even though none of the resource constraints changed.

Initial post

What to include

  1. List all vertices of the feasible region.
  2. Evaluate your chosen objective at every vertex in a table.
  3. State the maximizing vertex or vertices and the maximum objective value.
  4. If more than one vertex gives the same maximum, explain what that implies about the edge joining those vertices.
  5. Interpret your solution in terms of the decision variables rather than reporting only coordinates.
Replies

Engage with classmates’ mathematics

Respond to a classmate who used a different objective if possible. Explain why the feasible region stayed the same while the optimal solution changed—or, if their objective produced a tie, explain why an entire edge can be optimal.

A useful reply checks a calculation, compares methods or assumptions, or asks a mathematically specific question. Avoid replies that only say that you agree.
