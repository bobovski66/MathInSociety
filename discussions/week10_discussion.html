Week 10 / Discussion

Three calculations. Three misleading conclusions.

We have compared trajectories, forecasts, and geometric pieces. Choose a claim that goes further than its evidence and explain exactly where the reasoning fails.

Imagine reading these headlines

Imagine seeing these three claims after the Week 10 investigations. Each sounds reasonable until you check the actual mathematics.

Claim A · Collatz

“We tested a million Collatz starts. So every positive integer reaches one.”

Checking individual trajectories, even many of them, is different from proving a statement about every positive integer.

Claim B · Logistic map

“The forecast formula is exact, so a tiny measurement error cannot affect long-term predictions.”

Two almost identical starts can lead to very different long-term outputs under the same deterministic rule.

Claim C · Cantor set

“The Cantor construction has more pieces at every stage, so the total remaining length must be increasing.”

At every stage the number of pieces doubles, but each piece becomes one third as long.

None of these headlines follows from the evidence as stated. Your task is to explain why, using one calculation or observation that another reader could check.

Your initial post

Choose one headline to investigate in detail. Use the reading, the explorer, and your own calculations. Address all four parts below.

  1. State the mathematical rule. Explain how its next step is determined. Specify the starting number or starting state you're using so another person can reproduce your work.
  2. Produce evidence. If you chose Collatz, compare starts 8 and 9 and report the two stopping times. If you chose the logistic map, compare starts 0.4000 and 0.4001 at r = 3.9; calculate their difference at steps 10 and 15 using the reading's table or the explorer. If you chose the Cantor set, calculate the number of surviving pieces and their combined length at stage 5. Show enough computation for another student to check it.
  3. Correct the headline. Explain which part of the headline is not justified. Use the appropriate mathematical term—computational complexity, sensitive dependence, or self-similarity—and distinguish what your example demonstrates from what it does not establish.
  4. Make the idea useful. Describe a real situation where confusing these conclusions could lead someone to make a poor decision. You might consider a large computer search, a forecast based on imperfect measurements, or interpreting the results of a geometric construction. What additional evidence or care would help?

You do not need a general proof about Collatz or chaos. You do need to show one specific calculation, say what it supports, and explain what it does not establish. Submit your post on the class discussion board.

Reply to a classmate

Choose a post about a different headline from your own. Check one of its mathematical details, then explain how their example differs from yours. What did the two examples teach you about interpreting the results of repeated rules? Ask one question that could strengthen their conclusion.
