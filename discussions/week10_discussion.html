Week 10 / Discussion

Three simple rules. Three misleading headlines.

A calculation can be correct while a conclusion drawn from it goes too far. Choose a claim, use mathematics to investigate it, and explain what the evidence actually supports.

Imagine reading these headlines

Three articles describe research on recursive rules. Each article makes a claim that sounds plausible but misses an important distinction.

Claim A · Collatz

“We tested many starting numbers. That proves every number eventually reaches one.”

The computation has many successful examples, but its conclusion is about all positive integers.

Claim B · Logistic map

“The formula is exact. Therefore, a tiny measurement error can never matter.”

The experiment compares two almost identical starts under the same mathematical rule.

Claim C · Sierpiński

“The design has more and more triangles. Therefore, more and more area must be filled.”

The drawing is constructed by repeatedly removing the center of each surviving triangle.

None of these headlines follows from the evidence as stated. Your task is to explain why, using one calculation or observation that another reader could check.

Your initial post

Choose one headline to investigate in detail. Use the reading, the explorer, and your own calculations. Address all four parts below.

  1. State the mathematical rule. Explain how its next step is determined. Specify the starting number or starting state you're using so another person can reproduce your work.
  2. Produce evidence. If you chose Collatz, try a start of 9 and report its number of updates and peak. If you chose the logistic map, compare starts 0.4000 and 0.4001 at r = 3.9 and describe how their difference changes over 30 updates. If you chose Sierpiński, calculate the number of surviving triangles and fraction of area at stage 5. Show the calculation or cite a specific observed result from the explorer.
  3. Correct the headline. Explain which part of the headline is not justified. Use the appropriate mathematical term—computational complexity, sensitive dependence, or self-similarity—and distinguish what your example demonstrates from what it does not establish.
  4. Make the idea useful. Describe a real situation where confusing these conclusions could lead someone to make a poor decision. You might consider a large computer search, a forecast based on imperfect measurements, or a model of a complicated boundary. What additional evidence or care would help?

You do not need to solve an open mathematical problem or prove that the logistic map is chaotic. You need to show a specific calculation and support a careful interpretation. Submit your post on the class discussion board.

Reply to a classmate

Choose a post about a different headline from your own. Check one of its mathematical details, then explain how their example differs from yours. What did the two examples teach you about interpreting the results of repeated rules? Ask one question that could strengthen their conclusion.
