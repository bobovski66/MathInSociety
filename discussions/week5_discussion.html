The discussion / Explain a mechanism with evidence

When a school acts on last week’s report

A tutoring program uses an invented numerical rule to aim for mastery score 80. The modeled starting score is 56. Compare current progress reports with reports that arrive one week late. Work the calculations before reading classmates’ posts, then use them to explain how the timing of a report can affect an action.

This is a model of feedback, not a claim that teachers can add a fixed number of score points by assigning practice. Real learning has many influences; a score is a limited measurement. Explain both what the model reveals and a feature of education it omits.

Part A / Two rules, one changed assumption

Build the sequences through week 4

\[\text{next score}=\text{current score}+0.5(80-\text{reported score}).\]

For current reports, use \(x_{n+1}=x_n+0.5(80-x_n)\), with \(x_0=56\). For delayed reports, use \(x_{n+1}=x_n+0.5(80-x_{n-1})\), with \(x_{-1}=x_0=56\). Each step is one week.

Show how week 1 was obtained

Both arrangements begin with current state 56 and report 56, so the correction is 0.5(80 − 56) = 12. Both give 68. At the next update, their starting states still agree but their reports differ. That is where the sequences separate.

Part B / Interpret the loop

Who observes, decides, and acts?

Identify the modeled state, assessment measurement, reference 80, decision rule, and changed instruction or practice. Show how an action changes the conditions that generate the next measurement.

Then distinguish an old report from an easy assessment. A late report may describe an earlier week accurately; an unusually easy quiz may provide an unrepresentative measure of current mastery. Averaging several assessments can help only when their design and context make the comparison meaningful.

Part C / Observe the observer

Measuring a class can also change its behavior

Mead’s 1967 address, published in 1968, asked the American Society for Cybernetics to apply cybernetic ideas to its own operation. Read her feature in the history timeline, then consider how students might give feedback about what an assessment measures.
