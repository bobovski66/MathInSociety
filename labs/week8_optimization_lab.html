Week 8 / Optimization Extension

What would you recommend?

Use land, volunteer hours, donation budgets, and community programs to find feasible choices and explain why you recommend one.

From a calculation to a recommendation

In the reading, the bike shop had to decide how to use the time it had. Here you will face several other kinds of decisions: a farm planning one season, a library planning programs, and a community group purchasing supplies. Each has limits and an objective, but the details of what matters are different.

Use the stated numbers as simplified estimates. Find the feasible choices, compare their outcomes, and explain what a result would mean to the people making the decision. You may use fractional acreage, but meetings and bundles usually come in whole numbers.

Part 1 / Translate a resource table

Making a planting plan for the season

A small farm has fifty acres available for corn and soybeans. During a particular planting window, its crew has 120 labor-hours for preparing and planting the fields. Each acre of corn needs three hours of this work; each acre of soybeans needs two. The farmer estimates that each acre will contribute $200 or $150, respectively, toward the farm's income after the per-acre costs of growing that crop. Let \(x\) be acres of corn and \(y\) acres of soybeans. How can the farm use its land and planting time while earning the most under these estimates?

Ten acres of corn require \(3(10)=30\) hours of the planting work. Find the total hours for both crops and compare them with 120. Include \(x\geq0\) and \(y\geq0\), since planting negative acreage has no meaning.

Part 2 / A complete maximum

How many people can the library reach?

A community library has volunteers and supplies for two kinds of activities. A neighborhood information table (\(x\)) uses one materials kit and three volunteer-hours and typically reaches five people. A children's reading circle (\(y\)) uses two kits and two volunteer-hours and reaches eight. The library has sixteen kits and twenty-four volunteer-hours. How many people could it reach by arranging these activities?

\[\text{Maximize }f=5x+8y\quad\text{subject to }x+2y\leq16,\quad3x+2y\leq24,\quad x,y\geq0.\]

The supply limit is \(x+2y\leq16\), while the volunteer-time limit is \(3x+2y\leq24\). The estimated number of people reached is \(f=5x+8y\). Start with the intersection of the boundary lines, then check all four feasible corners before recommending a schedule. The two equations have the same \(2y\) term, so subtracting them is helpful.

Part 3 / Requirements instead of allowances

Buying supplies for a community program

A volunteer group is preparing supplies for a youth program. A store sells two prepacked donation bags. Bag A contains one folder and two blank cards for $3; bag B contains one folder and one card for $5. The group needs at least eight folders and ten cards. Let \(x\) count A-bags and \(y\) count B-bags. The group wants to meet both needs for the lowest cost.

\[\text{Minimize }C=3x+5y,\qquad x+y\geq8,\quad2x+y\geq10,\quad x,y\geq0.\]

This time the limits are minimum requirements: the group must buy at least enough. The shaded feasible region extends beyond the drawing because extra bags are allowed. Calculate the cost at the three boundary corners and choose the least expensive option. As you work, notice whether one bag offers better value than the other under the assumptions given.

Part 4 / Keep the allowed set fixed

The community center has more than one goal

A community center is deciding between two kinds of events: \(x\) adult learning workshops and \(y\) family reading sessions. The staff have already determined that the feasible corner schedules are (0,0), (8,0), (5,4), and (0,6). Each workshop is expected to welcome ten people, raise $15 in donations, and require $4 in supplies. Each family session is expected to welcome twelve people, raise $8, and use $6 in supplies. Compare three different measures: attendance \(A=10x+12y\), donations \(R=15x+8y\), and supply cost \(C=4x+6y\).

A question about cost needs a service requirement

The center wants to hold events, so compare costs only for the three nonzero corner schedules. If we allowed doing nothing and asked only for the lowest cost, what would happen? A minimum-cost decision needs a statement about what the program must provide.

Part 5 / An optimal edge

Several schedules may serve equally many people

A neighborhood group can schedule \(x\) open-house sessions or \(y\) small-group workshops. Each is expected to serve three people. Its staffing and room availability give a feasible region with corners (0,0), (4,0), (3,2), and (0,5), in that order. The group wants to serve as many people as possible, so maximize \(f=3x+3y\). The equal coefficients mean that each additional event contributes three expected participants.

Before making a recommendation

Check the units of each result, name the people or resources affected, and explain which assumptions a real decision-maker would want to verify.
