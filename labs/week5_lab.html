Week 5 Laboratory — Cybernetic Systems

Build recursive feedback sequences, compare immediate and delayed measurements, graph oscillation, and connect control decisions to statistics.

Why feedback matters

A cybernetic system does more than react once: it repeatedly observes its condition, compares that observation with a desired condition, and changes what happens next. The new state is measured again. This recurring loop is how a thermostat regulates temperature, how a person adjusts their balance, and how some computer systems learn from errors.

In this lab, you will follow the same loop in several settings. The numbers are deliberately manageable so you can focus on what each number means.

Before you begin

The central pattern this week is:

state
measurement

The arithmetic is simple on purpose. The important skill is recognizing what the numbers mean inside a loop.

Bridge from Module 1 · A sequence of decisions

Start with a sequence

In Module 1, you learned how a rule turns one number into the next. For example, starting at 10 and subtracting 2 gives the sequence 10, 8, 6, 4, .... Feedback is also a recursive process: a controller observes a value, chooses a correction, and produces the next value.

For our greenhouse, \(x_0=64\) is the starting temperature, and the target is \(70^\circ\mathrm F\). The controller changes its correction each round because the remaining difference changes. Later, we will introduce a one-step delay: the controller acts on last round's measurement instead of today's.

Our question: Can a controller trying to reach 70°F go too far and start swinging from one side of 70°F to the other? Yes—and we can discover why with a table of just a few steps.

Part 1 · Find the loop

What is being observed and changed?

Every control loop has a state (what the system is like right now), a reference or target (what it should be like), a way to measure the state, and an action that can change it. The observation itself is not the action: a thermometer can measure the room, but a heater changes its temperature.

Think it through: In a shower, the water temperature you feel is the measurement, your preferred temperature is the target, and turning the handle is the action. You assess the new temperature and adjust again.

A building uses a thermostat to keep a classroom near 70°F. The room is currently 64°F. The thermostat measures the room and turns on the heat.

Part 2 · Error and correction

Run a feedback loop by hand

A simple numerical controller measures its error, defined as target minus current state. It then corrects a fraction of that error. A response strength of \(k=0.5\) means that the controller corrects half of the remaining difference at each step—not half of the original difference forever.

Worked example: A system starts at 20 with target 28 and \(k=0.25\). The error is \(28-20=8\). The correction is \(0.25(8)=2\), giving new state 22. Now the remaining error is \(28-22=6\), and the next correction is \(1.5\), giving state 23.5. Notice how the correction changes as the system approaches its goal.

Use target \(r=70\), starting state \(x_0=64\), and response strength \(k=0.5\).

error = target − observed,    next state = current state + k(error)
Part 3 · A one-step delay

What if the information is one round old?

In Part 2, the controller used the current temperature. Now change just one thing: every reading takes a full round to arrive. The controller still changes the current temperature, but it uses the temperature from the previous step to decide the correction.

new state = current state + 0.5(70 − previous state)

The starting temperature is \(x_0=64\). We also assume the temperature was 64 just before we began: \(x_{-1}=64\). That extra starting value makes the delayed rule possible.

Two worked steps: At step 0, the previous reading is 64: \(x_1=64+0.5(70-64)=67\). At step 1, the previous reading is still 64, while the actual current temperature is 67: \(x_2=67+0.5(70-64)=70\).

Continue the sequence. Each calculation uses a current state and the one-step-old value.

Part 4 · A sequence that oscillates

Continue, graph, and explain the back-and-forth motion

You just found that the delayed system reaches 70°F and then rises to 71.5°F. Why? At step 2, the controller was still using the old 67°F reading and decided more heat was needed.

Continue from \(x_4=71.5\). Remember: a one-step delay means that to find \(x_{n+1}\), we use the older reading \(x_{n-1}\), not \(x_n\).

Part 5 · What statistics adds

Trustworthy information, arriving in time

Real thermometers sometimes give readings that vary, even when conditions stay about the same. A normal distribution and a standard deviation help us describe that variation. Noisy information may be wrong about the present, while delayed information may correctly describe a time that has already passed.

Suppose stable readings are approximately normal with mean 68°F and standard deviation 2°F. A new reading of 72°F arrives. Its z-score is \(z=(72-68)/2\).

Taking several readings may help reduce random measurement variation. But waiting for extra readings can make the controller act on older information.

Looking ahead: A message spreading from person to person and a computer learning from errors are also recursive feedback processes. Explore the AI application to see a sequence of predictions and weight updates, or the greenhouse application to make a control decision.
Part 6 · Make the connection to cybernetics

What makes a system cybernetic?

We have used sequences to see what delayed feedback does. Now identify the larger system behind those calculations. Cybernetics studies how machines, organisms, and social systems use information about their state to guide actions that influence what happens next.

In the greenhouse, the state is temperature, the sensor is the thermometer, the reference is 70°F, the controller compares the reading with that target, and the actuator is the heater or fan. The action changes the greenhouse, which produces a new reading.

Corrective (negative) feedback acts against a departure, as when a heater warms a cold room. Amplifying (positive) feedback reinforces a change, as when increased attention leads to more sharing. Adaptive feedback changes later behavior using the outcome of an earlier attempt. These words describe what the loop does, not whether its results are good or bad.
