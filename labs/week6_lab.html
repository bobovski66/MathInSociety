Week 6 / Laboratory

Growth, interest, and loan payments

Explore how money changes over time, from a fixed annual increase to reinvested interest and monthly loan payments.

About this investigation

Suppose you are deciding what to do with money over several years. You might leave it in a savings account, compare offers that calculate interest differently, or borrow money and make monthly payments. In every case, one period’s ending balance becomes the next period’s starting point. The rule used to update that balance determines what happens over time.

We will begin with a fixed yearly addition, move to growth that builds on previous growth, and finish by following a loan payment through several months. Work through the sections in order: each introduces an idea used in the next.

Use a calculator when needed. Write money amounts to the nearest cent, but keep extra decimal places during compound-interest calculations. For numerical percentage answers, enter 4 to mean 4%; for an interest rate inside a calculation, use its decimal form, such as 0.04. In the loan ledger, round each month's interest to cents before computing the new balance.

Part 1 · A fixed amount each year

What happens when interest stays the same?

Imagine an account that calculates its interest from the original deposit each year. This is called simple interest. A $500 deposit at 3% simple annual interest earns $15 every year, because \(500\times0.03=15\). After two years, the account holds $530: the original $500 plus two additions of $15.

If \(P\) is the initial deposit, \(r\) the annual rate written as a decimal, and \(t\) the number of years, then the balance is \(B=P(1+rt)\). Notice that the yearly addition, \(Pr\), is calculated from the original deposit.

Use the rule to predict a balance

For each account, first find the interest earned in one year. Multiply that amount by the number of years, then add the original deposit.

Work backward from the ending balance

Sometimes you know how much the account has grown but need to recover the rate or the time. Find the total increase first. Since the yearly interest is constant, divide the total increase by the appropriate number of years or by the fixed yearly addition.

Connect the calculation to the growth pattern

Consider a third account starting at $1,200 with 5% simple interest. Find its constant yearly increase before finding its six-year balance.

The account is growing, but its interest is not increasing. Explain that distinction using the original balance and the annual interest amount you found.

Part 2 · Growth builds on growth

What changes when interest is reinvested?

Now consider a savings account that leaves each year's interest in the account. The next interest calculation uses the new, larger balance. For example, $500 earning 10% annually becomes $550 after one year, then $605 after the second year. The second year's $55 of interest is larger because the starting balance has changed.

This process is called compound interest. With annual compounding, the account is multiplied by \(1+r\) each year, giving \(B=P(1+r)^t\). With monthly compounding, use the monthly rate \(r/12\) and apply that update \(12t\) times: \(B=P(1+r/12)^{12t}\).

Follow the repeated update

Calculate the first two accounts using annual updates. For the third, identify the monthly percentage and the total number of months before evaluating the expression. Round only the final balances.

Compare three rules under the same conditions

Suppose you deposit $1,500 for 10 years at a quoted annual rate of 6%. Compare simple interest, annual compounding, and monthly compounding. By holding the principal, quoted rate, and time fixed, you can isolate the effect of the growth rule itself.

Look at the difference between the simple-interest and compound-interest results. Think about which amount earns interest in the second year, and why the difference can accumulate over ten years.

Part 3 · How often is interest added?

Does the frequency of compounding matter?

A bank might advertise a 6% nominal annual rate while adding interest annually, monthly, or daily. The quoted rate stays fixed, but the size and number of the updates change. If interest is added \(n\) times per year, the amount after \(t\) years is \(B=P(1+r/n)^{nt}\).

For this comparison, use $1,000 at a nominal annual rate of 6% for 10 years. Use \(n=1\) for annual, \(n=12\) for monthly, and \(n=365\) for daily compounding. Treat a year as exactly 365 days for this exercise.

First determine the update schedule

The exponent counts how many times the interest rule is applied. The periodic interest rate is the nominal annual rate divided by the number of periods in a year.

Calculate and compare the balances

Apply the same principal, nominal rate, and ten-year duration to each schedule. Keep the full calculator value during the computation and round your reported balance to cents.

Translate the quoted rate into an actual one-year gain

The effective annual yield describes the percentage increase after one year, taking compounding into account. For monthly compounding, calculate \( (1+r/12)^{12}-1 \), then multiply by 100 to express the result as a percentage. Use this to investigate a different quoted rate.

Part 4 · Thinking over longer periods

How long does it take an amount to double?

Compound growth can be hard to estimate mentally, particularly over many years. The Rule of 72 gives a quick approximation: divide 72 by the annual percentage growth rate to estimate the number of years required for an amount to double. For instance, a rate of 9% suggests a doubling time of about \(72/9=8\) years.

This is an approximation, not a promise about an investment. In this section, assume the growth rate remains constant and that all growth is retained.

Estimate doubling times

Use the stated annual percentage directly in the Rule of 72. A larger annual rate should correspond to a shorter doubling time.

Reverse the estimate, then extend it

You can also work backward from a desired doubling time to estimate the required annual rate. To reach four times the starting amount, an account must double and then double again; under constant proportional growth, those two doubling intervals take the same amount of time.

So far, balances have increased because we kept adding interest. A loan adds a new ingredient: money leaves the balance through scheduled payments. The final section follows this combination one month at a time.

Part 5 · Interest and payments together

Follow a loan from one month to the next

Suppose you borrow $3,000 and agree to pay $150 at the end of each month. The loan charges 9% nominal annual interest, calculated monthly on the amount still owed. Each month, the lender first adds interest; then your payment reduces the amount owed. Next month, the interest is calculated from the new balance.

The monthly interest rate is \(0.09/12=0.0075\), or 0.75%. Use this update rule:

\(\text{new balance}=\text{starting balance}+\text{monthly interest}-150\)

For a smaller example, consider a $1,000 loan at 12% annually with a $100 monthly payment. The first month's rate is 1%, so interest is $10. After the payment, the balance is $910. In the next month, interest is calculated on $910, giving $9.10. We will apply the same sequence to the $3,000 loan.

Keep a running record of the balance. In every month, calculate the interest from that month's starting balance, round the interest to cents, add it to what is owed, and subtract $150. The ending balance becomes the next month's starting balance.

Begin with $3,000. First find 0.75% of that amount. Then add the interest and subtract $150. Keep your ending balance for the next step.

Month 2: use your previous answer

The amount left at the end of month 1 is now the starting balance. Repeat the interest calculation and payment, using that smaller starting balance.

Months 3 and 4: keep the ledger moving

Continue carrying each ending balance forward. Watch how the interest charge changes from month to month, even though the interest rate and payment stay the same.

Understand where the payment goes

Part of each $150 payment covers that month's interest. The rest reduces the amount originally borrowed, called the principal. Compare the first payment with its interest charge to find the reduction in principal.

For the two hypothetical loans, the first month's interest is based on the same initial balance. Once different payments are made, the loans end that month with different balances.
