About this investigation

When a measurement varies across a population, we can ask two related questions: where an individual observation falls in the distribution, and what a sample tells us about an unknown population quantity. Those questions need different mathematical tools.

Part 2. Z-scores

A score of 85 has no fixed meaning without knowing the scale of the measurement. A z-score expresses its position in standard-deviation units, making observations from different distributions comparable.

Part 3. Parameter or statistic?

Pollsters and researchers measure a sample because observing the entire population is often impractical. The unknown feature of the full population is a parameter; the number calculated from the observations is a statistic.

In each study, identify the larger group of interest and the information actually reported from the sample. The sample result is an estimate, and its usefulness depends partly on how the sampled group was chosen.

Part 4. Confidence intervals

A sample estimate is rarely exact. A confidence interval combines a point estimate with a margin of error to give a range of plausible values for a population parameter. An estimate of 50 with a margin of 3 gives an interval from 47 to 53.

For each example, subtract and add the stated margin. Then interpret the resulting interval as a statement about the population quantity being estimated, not a range into which all individual measurements must fall.

Part 5. Precision and tool choice

A larger sample often produces a more precise estimate. Under comparable conditions, a typical margin of error scales roughly like 1 divided by the square root of sample size. That relationship explains why cutting the margin in half requires considerably more observations.
