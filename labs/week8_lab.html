Week 8 / Laboratory

Choosing what fits

A good plan has to fit the time and people available. This lab uses familiar community decisions to explore what is possible and what is worth choosing.

Before you begin

The neighborhood bike shop in the reading had to balance the number of bikes it could accept with the hours its mechanics could work. The same problem comes up when a community center schedules activities, volunteers give rides, or an outreach group decides how to spend its time.

Each part of this lab tells you what two quantities mean. Keep that setting in mind as you calculate: a point is a possible plan, a constraint is a limit the people involved face, and an objective expresses what they hope to accomplish.

Part 1 / The segment test

Would an in-between plan still work?

Imagine comparing two workable schedules at a community center. If you divide staff time between them, does every in-between schedule also work? For choices that can be divided, such as hours or acreage, that question leads to convexity: a region is convex when the straight segment between any two of its points stays in the region. For individual bikes or people, points between whole-number schedules can be understood as averages across days.

Treat each filled shape as a collection of allowed choices. Pick two points inside it and picture the straight path between them. Does the path always stay inside? One segment that leaves the shape is enough to show that the region is not convex.

Classify the regions below. In the half-plane example, \(x\geq2\) might mean a program must schedule at least two activities.

Part 2 / A boundary and its side

How much can fit into a community center's afternoon?

A community center has eight room-hours available for an afternoon of programs. A short drop-in activity needs one hour; a workshop needs two. Let \(x\) count drop-ins and \(y\) count workshops. The total room time must satisfy \(x+2y\leq8\). The boundary \(x+2y=8\) describes schedules that use every available hour.

To see the boundary, find the two schedules that use all eight hours with only one kind of activity. Set one variable to zero at a time. Then check whether the sample schedules fit within the available time.

Use \(x+2y\leq8\) to answer the questions. For each point, read the coordinates as numbers of drop-ins and workshops.

Part 3 / Give the coefficients units

Planning rides with volunteer drivers

A community ride program has sixty volunteer driver-hours available this week. A longer ride takes three driver-hours and a local ride takes two. Let \(x\) count longer rides and \(y\) count local rides. A schedule uses \(3x+2y\) driver-hours and must satisfy \(3x+2y\leq60\).

First find how many rides could be offered if the program arranged only one type. Then check two mixed schedules. A point such as (10,10) means ten longer rides and ten local rides, not ten or twenty hours.

Think about the units: \(3\) driver-hours per longer ride, multiplied by \(x\) rides, gives \(3x\) driver-hours. The same reasoning gives \(2y\) hours for local rides. The sum must fit the sixty hours available.

Part 4 / The overlap

Find the schedules a small bike clinic could manage

A volunteer bike clinic is open for a shorter community event. It can accept up to seven bicycles and has ten mechanic-hours available. A tune-up takes two hours and a quick repair takes one. If \(x\) counts tune-ups and \(y\) counts quick repairs, which combinations can the clinic finish?

The clinic has two limits: \(2x+y\leq10\) for mechanic time and \(x+y\leq7\) for the number of bikes. The feasible region is the overlap. Find where the two boundary lines meet, then check the axis intercepts. A corner only counts if it respects both limits.

\[2x+y\leq10,\qquad x+y\leq7,\qquad x,y\geq0.\]

Find every feasible corner below. You can use the feasible-region explorer to check your graph: choose the lab model. Notice which resource is exhausted at each corner.

Part 5 / One region, two objectives

The best schedule depends on the goal

A neighborhood outreach team has two ways to spend its time: \(x\) is the number of drop-in advice sessions, and \(y\) is the number of neighborhood outreach rounds. Each advice session reaches about three people face to face and results in one follow-up action. Each outreach round reaches about two people face to face and leads to four follow-up actions. Both kinds of work matter, but they produce different results.

The team has already worked out the feasible corner schedules from its staffing and travel limits. We will compare two goals: maximize people spoken to directly, \(f=3x+2y\), or maximize follow-up actions, \(g=x+4y\). Calculate each measure at every corner before recommending a schedule.

The feasible corners are (0,0), (6,0), (4,3), and (0,5). Keep the coordinates in that order: advice sessions first, outreach rounds second.

f = 3x + 2y

g = x + 4y
