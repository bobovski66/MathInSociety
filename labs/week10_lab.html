Week 10 / Guided laboratory

When a simple rule leads somewhere unexpected

Investigate what Collatz takes to compute, what the logistic map can predict, and what happens to a triangle when its construction repeats. Each part has calculations and a short explanation.

In each part, first apply the rule to a few specific values. Then explain what the calculation tells us. The Week 10 explorer can check your work, but write down the essential steps so another person could follow your reasoning.

Step 0 is the starting state. Five updates create six values, counting the start. You may enter fractions such as 27/64 for numerical answers, or an equivalent decimal. Your responses save in this browser; the downloaded HTML copy is useful for submission.

Part 1 · Complexity

One step is easy. How many steps are required?

The rule is: divide by two if the number is even; multiply by three and add one if it is odd. Starting at 6, the values are

6 → 3 → 10 → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1

This route reaches 1 after eight updates, and its peak is 16. Try the rule with 10, then use the explorer to compare other starting values.

Now think of a different kind of computation: a balanced decision tree with two possible choices at each step. After four decisions it has 16 possible four-bit addresses, as we saw in Week 9.

Part 2 · Chaos

The same equation can behave differently

Use the logistic update (x_{n+1}=r x_n(1-x_n)). The variable (x) is a fraction between 0 and 1. Keep all digits during intermediate calculations and round only when reporting.

One update

For (r=2.8) and (x_0=0.2), the first update is (x_1=2.8(0.2)(0.8)=0.448).

Open the logistic-map section of the explorer. Select (r=2.8), then (r=3.2), then (r=3.9). Leave the starting value and number of updates unchanged while making your comparison.

Finally, for (r=3.9), compare starts (x_0=0.4000) and (y_0=0.4001). The two trajectories use the same update rule.

Part 3 · Fractals

Count the pieces. Measure the remaining area.

Start with a filled triangle of area 1. At each stage, keep three corner triangles, each with half the side length of its parent. Each small triangle has one quarter of its parent's area, so three quarters of the total filled area remains.

At stage 3, each small triangle has side length one eighth of the original. This is one way to see the repeated scaling in the figure.
