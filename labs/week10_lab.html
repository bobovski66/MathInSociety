Week 10 / Guided laboratory

When a simple rule leads somewhere unexpected

Compare neighboring Collatz trajectories, two nearly identical logistic forecasts, and the changing pieces of a Cantor set. Calculate first, then explain what the evidence means.

In each part, first apply the rule to a few specific values. Then explain what the calculation tells us. The Week 10 explorer can check your work, but write down the essential steps so another person could follow your reasoning.

Step 0 is the starting state. Five updates create six values, counting the start. You may enter fractions such as 8/27 for numerical answers, or an equivalent decimal. Your responses save in this browser; the downloaded HTML copy is useful for submission.

Part 1 · Computation

Can neighboring starts require very different work?

Apply the Collatz rule: divide even numbers by 2, and replace odd numbers by 3n + 1. Stop at the first 1. Write both trajectories before entering your answers. Count arrows, not numbers.

For a start of 6: 6 → 3 → 10 → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1.

Part 2 · Prediction

Can a tiny measurement difference matter later?

Use the logistic rule \(\,x_{n+1}=3.9x_n(1-x_n)\,\). Compare two starts: 0.4000 and 0.4001. The same formula is applied to each new value independently.

Part 3 · Geometry

Why can there be more pieces but less length?

Take a segment of length 1 and remove its open middle third. At the next stage, remove the open middle third from each surviving segment. Repeat. Sketch the first three stages on paper, then complete the calculations.

In class, compare this with the Sierpiński triangle: the pieces triple at each stage, but the total filled area is multiplied by 3/4.
