Week 10 / Laboratory

Investigate the rule. Explain the pattern.

Trace, calculate, and compare: a guided laboratory on nearby starts, Collatz, fractal scaling, logistic dynamics, and observation scale.

About this investigation

Many systems are governed by simple rules applied repeatedly. Their long-term behavior can still be difficult to predict, especially when outcomes depend on the starting value, the number of updates, or the scale at which something is observed.

These five investigations use number sequences, geometric constructions, a population model, and a coastline survey. For every sequence, call the starting value step 0; five updates therefore produce six recorded values. Carry calculator precision forward between steps, rounding only answers you report. Part 1 / Nearby starts

Which difference are you tracking?

Suppose two forecasts begin with nearly the same value. Will that small difference remain small, grow steadily, or grow in proportion to the values themselves? The answer depends on the update rule.

The absolute difference is the numerical gap between the two outcomes. The relative difference measures that gap against a reference value. Apply the same number of updates to both starting points before comparing them. Keep addition and multiplication rules separate as you interpret the result.

The absolute difference is the distance between two values. The relative difference divides that distance by the magnitude of the first value.

Compare how two nearby starting values change under the same rule.

Part 2 / Collatz

Count arrows. Record the peak.

The Collatz rule is easy to state: halve an even integer; for an odd integer, multiply by three and add one. Repeating the rule creates a path of integers whose length and highest value are not obvious from its starting point.

Begin with each assigned value and write down every new term until the sequence first reaches 1. Count updates, not entries in the list; the original value is step 0. The peak is the largest value seen anywhere along that finite path.

Even values are divided by 2; odd values are multiplied by 3 and then increased by 1. Stop at the first 1. The peak includes the starting value and all values up to that first 1.

Apply the even or odd rule at each step until the sequence reaches 1.

Now trace the complete routes from 6 and 7. You can use the explorer to check each even/odd decision.

Part 3 / Fractal accounting

More pieces does not always mean more area

A geometric shape can become increasingly intricate by repeating one small construction. In the Koch process, each line segment is replaced by four segments, each one-third as long as the original.

Track two quantities separately: the number of pieces and their total length. The piece count multiplies by four at every stage; the total length multiplies by 4/3. Use the previous stage to find the next rather than trying to count a detailed picture by eye.

For each Koch stage, count the pieces and find their total length.

Sierpinski area

The Sierpinski triangle keeps three of four equal smaller triangles at each stage. Find the area remaining.

The initial area is 1. Each stage keeps three quarters of the previous area.

Part 4 / The logistic map

First an update; then a long-run pattern

A simple population model might predict next season's population fraction from this season's fraction. In the logistic update, x(1−x) reflects both the existing population and the limits on growth, while the parameter r controls the response.

Evaluate the recursion in sequence, always using the newest value. Distinguish a fixed point, which would remain unchanged if reached, from a trajectory that merely moves near one. Compare the long-run patterns produced by the parameter choices in the questions.

The state \(x_n\) is a fraction of a reference population. Use \(x_{n+1}=rx_n(1-x_n)\) to model one breeding season per update.

\(x_{n+1}=r x_n(1-x_n)\)

Part 5 / Measuring at a scale

The ruler is part of the report

Imagine tracing a jagged coastline using straight rulers. A long ruler misses smaller bends, while a short ruler can follow more of the outline. The reported length may therefore depend on the scale of measurement.

Calculate each row by multiplying ruler length by the number of placements. Then compare the estimates as the ruler becomes shorter. Ask what this tells you about measurements made on complicated natural boundaries.
