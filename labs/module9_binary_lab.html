Numbers, codes, and choices

A digital code can represent a number or a route. Begin by counting and calculating in binary and octal. Then use binary sequences to reach services in a decision tree.

About this lab. Show your calculations, including place values and carries or borrows. You may use the interactive explorer to check your answers after working them out. Your responses save automatically in this browser.

1. What do the digits mean?

When the base changes, the place values change. In binary the places from right to left are 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, … . In octal they are 1, 8, 64, 512, … . A small subscript tells us which base we're using.

Binary example. 101012 = 16 + 4 + 1 = 2110. To write 1010 in binary, use 8 + 2, giving 10102.
Octal example. 1538 means one group of 64, five groups of 8, and three ones. So 1538 = 64 + 40 + 3 = 10710. To write 4010 in octal, use five eights and no ones: 508.

2. Carrying and borrowing in a different base

The arithmetic is familiar: line up equal place values, begin at the right, and regroup when a column has enough units to make a group in the next place. In binary, a carry happens at two. In octal, it happens at eight.

Binary addition. 10112 + 01102 = 100012. The rightmost column gives 1; each of the next three columns makes a carry. In decimal, 11 + 6 = 17.
Binary subtraction. 11012 − 00112 = 10102. To subtract 1 from the empty twos column, borrow one four, which becomes two twos. Check: 13 − 3 = 10.
Octal addition. 578 + 268 = 1058. In the ones column, 7 + 6 = 13, so write 5 and carry one eight. In the eights column, 5 + 2 + 1 = 8, giving another carry. Check: 47 + 22 = 69.
Octal subtraction. 708 − 258 = 438. Borrowing one eight gives eight ones, so the ones column yields 8 − 5 = 3. The eights column is now 6 − 2 = 4.

A useful shortcut: three bits make one octal digit

Since 8 = 2 × 2 × 2, group a binary number into three-bit blocks starting from the right. For example, 1101012 becomes 110 | 101, or 658. In the other direction, replace each octal digit with its three-bit binary equivalent.

3. A binary string can be an address

A bit string doesn't always stand for a number. In a decision tree, 0 means go left and 1 means go right. The first digit determines the first choice; later digits give the next choices in order.

Address example. 10 means right, then left. Extending it by another choice gives 100 or 101. Here the digits give directions, not a quantity to add.

4. A short program tells us when to stop

The community center routes calls to pantry hours, bus times, regular ride help, and urgent ride help. Each caller follows the 0/1 choices until a service is reached.

Example. Code 10 reaches bus times after two questions. The caller stops at that service rather than continuing to answer questions.

5. Does the menu save callers time?

The center receives sixteen calls: eight for pantry hours, four for bus times, two for regular ride help, and two for urgent rides. The lengths of the four service codes are 1, 2, 3, and 3 questions respectively.

Example. The eight pantry callers answer 8 × 1 = 8 questions. The four bus callers answer 4 × 2 = 8 questions. The total for all callers counts these contributions along with the ride requests.

Fewer questions overall may still mean more steps for someone seeking urgent help. Which measure would matter most if you were designing the menu for your community?

6. A rule can remove possible codes

Suppose the system forbids two ones next to each other. That removes 11 from the two-digit addresses. Among three-digit addresses, only 000, 001, 010, 100, and 101 remain allowed.

7. Think about a decision system you know

Phone menus, forms, and websites all present choices that can guide people toward different results. Describe a familiar example and explain what makes its choices easy or difficult to follow. Could a short code or a small decision tree capture what matters in that situation?
