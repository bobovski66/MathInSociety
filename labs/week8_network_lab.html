Investigating connections

A community depends on connections. In this lab, you will find routes, test what happens when one fails, and decide where a new connection would help.

Start here. You can complete this lab while learning the ideas. Read the short example in each section, follow its instructions, and write your answers. The Network Explorer helps you test changes; you do not need to read the entire chapter first. Your answers save automatically in this browser. When finished, print or download your work.

1. Who works with whom?

Five organizations share information about food donations: a pantry (P), library (L), school (S), community fridge (F), and senior hub (H). The lines show direct, two-way partnerships. A dot represents an organization; a line between two dots represents one partnership.

Direct partnerships: P–L, P–S, L–S, S–F, F–H.

Example — count the pantry's partners. Trace the lines touching P. They lead to L and S, so P has 2 direct partners. Mathematicians call that count its degree. An indirect contact does not add to the degree.

Try it: First trace the lines from S. Then do the same for every organization. Count each line only once when you count the total number of partnerships.

2. How can an alert get across the county?

The county uses seven locations, A through G. A line represents a direct two-way communication connection. You may travel along the lines, but you cannot skip across a missing connection. A route is a sequence of connected locations. Its length is the number of lines traveled, not the number of letters written.

The county's original communication network. Every line is one possible direct contact.

Example — A to D. A can send to B, and B can send to D. The route A–B–D uses 2 links. A–C–D works too. The picture does not show a direct A–D link.

Try it: Start at A and follow drawn lines to G. Count each segment. See whether you can find a route using fewer links. If you want help tracing, open the explorer, select A and G, and choose Highlight shortest path.

What does “shortest” mean? So far, every connection counts as one step. Real walking routes may take different amounts of time. A three-segment walk can be faster than a two-segment walk.

3. What if an important link stops working?

Sometimes one failed connection separates an entire network. A group of locations that can still reach one another is called a connected component. If there are two components, you cannot send a message between them using the remaining links.

Example — losing B–C. Even without the direct B–C connection, B can reach C along B–A–C. The route has changed, but the locations can still communicate.

Do this in the explorer: (1) Choose Reset all changes. (2) Tick D–E has failed. (3) Look at the reported number of connected groups and the groups of letters shown below the drawing. The dashed D–E line is unavailable.

4. You can pay for one new connection. Which should it be?

The county can add one direct contact. The goal is specific: even if D–E is down, A must still be able to reach G. The county has two proposals: add A–D, or add D–F.

How to compare the proposals. Keep D–E has failed checked in the explorer. First choose A–D: short local contact from Add one contact, and read Connected groups and Shortest path. Then switch to D–F: alternate regional contact and look again. A proposal succeeds only if a route from A to G exists while D–E is down.

One successful repair does not make a system safe against every failure. For example, what happens if location D itself goes offline, taking its connections with it?

5. How quickly can an alert spread?

Return to the original network. Only A has the alert at the beginning, called round 0. In each new round, every informed location passes it to its direct neighbors. New recipients wait until the following round to forward it. A location stays informed after receiving the message.

Example — the first round. At round 0, only A is informed, so the total is 1. In round 1, B and C receive the message for the first time. That's 2 newly informed and 3 informed in total (A, B, and C).

Final reflection: a connection you know

Pick one real system you have seen or used: bus stops, school communication, food deliveries, walking paths, community groups, or something similar. Choose a few places or people that would be represented by dots. Explain what a line between them would mean. Then name one practical question the network could help answer and one important detail the simple drawing leaves out.

For instance: In a bus network, dots might be stops and lines might represent direct service between stops. A question is whether a rider can still get to a clinic after a route closes. A drawing that counts links alone would leave out waiting times and wheelchair access.
