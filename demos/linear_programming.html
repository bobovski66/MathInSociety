Keep the region and the goal in view

The constraints say which plans are possible. The objective gives each plan a score. Start with the snack-pack example, then change the objective coefficients without changing the limits. A large x coefficient rewards x more strongly per unit; it does not remove the resources needed to produce x.

The table lists actual feasible vertices, not corners created by the drawing window. For an unbounded region, the optimizer also checks whether an allowed direction keeps improving the score.

Optional / Whole-number comparison

When the dots are the allowable choices

This comparison is available for small bounded models only. It enumerates feasible integer points; it does not round the continuous optimum and assume the result is best. A black diamond marks a best whole-number plan when this comparison is on.

Four useful experiments

  1. For snack packs, begin at 3x+2y. Change the coefficients to x+4y. Which corner becomes best? Which part of the model stayed fixed?
  2. Use x+y on the snack region. Two vertices tie. Test (2,6) on their connecting edge.
  3. Choose the minimum-requirements blend. Keep positive costs and compare minimization with maximization. Why does an unbounded feasible region give different kinds of answers to those questions?
  4. Choose fractional versus whole batches and enable the whole-number comparison. Compare the continuous score 33, rounded-down score 29, and best whole-number score 32.

What a tie does—and does not—say

Equal scores at two vertices are useful only after verifying they are best. If both coefficients are zero, every feasible point has score zero. If an unbounded direction has no effect on an optimal score, there may be an unbounded family of tied plans. The result statement distinguishes these cases.
