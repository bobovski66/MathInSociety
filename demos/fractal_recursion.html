Week 10 / Explorer

A workshop for simple rules

Compare nearby trajectories, trace every Collatz update, and watch length and area respond to repeated geometric rules.

Four investigationsGraphs + numerical tables

Predict first. Change one thing. Compare.

Choose an investigation below. Each one includes labeled graphics and a numerical table. A plotted pattern is evidence about the calculation shown; use the reading to distinguish a numerical experiment from a general mathematical claim.

In every sequence, the starting value is at update 0. Decimals in tables are rounded for display; calculations retain full stored precision.

A first route through the workshop

  1. Begin with Logistic trajectories. Use the fixed-point preset to see two forecasts settle together.
  2. Try the two-cycle preset, then the sensitive-pair preset. Look for the difference between settling, repeating, and separating.
  3. Visit Collatz routes to study an arithmetic rule, then Fractal workshop to study a geometric replacement rule.
  4. Finish with Rulers & scale, where the ruler changes while the boundary stays the same.

“State” means the model’s current value; “update” means one use of its rule. The reading introduces each idea before the laboratory asks you to use it.

Investigation 1

One formula, two starting measurements

Imagine a population written as a fraction of a reference population. A state of 0.40 means 40% of that reference; a state of 0.80 means 80%. One update can represent one breeding season. This is a simplified setting for exploring the rule, not a fitted forecast for a real population.

The letter \(r\) is the model’s response setting. \(x_n\) is the current population fraction, and \(1-x_n\) is a limiting factor. With \(r=2\) and \(x_n=0.2\), for example, the next fraction is \(2(0.2)(0.8)=0.32\).

\(x_{n+1}=r x_n(1-x_n)\)

The solid line starts at A. The dashed line starts at B = A + the initial gap. The rule and parameter are identical for both. Before choosing a preset, predict whether the trajectories will settle together, settle into a cycle, or separate substantially.

Start here: at r = 2.8 these nearby forecasts settle toward the same value. Then try the two-cycle and sensitive-pair presets.

What each control changes

  • Parameter r: the setting used at every update of both forecasts.
  • Starting state A: the first estimate of the starting fraction.
  • Initial gap: the amount added to A to make starting state B. With A = 0.4000 and a gap of 0.0001, B = 0.4001.
  • Updates: how many times to apply the rule. Row 0 in the table is the starting information.

The first-gap readout uses a cutoff of 0.05: five percentage points of the reference population. It reports the first update when the two predicted fractions differ by more than that amount. “None in 40 updates” describes the displayed run.

Use the presets as three experiments

Fixed point: inspect the last ten values; they approach one common value. Two-cycle: inspect the last ten values for an alternating high-low pattern. Sensitive pair: watch initially close forecasts later separate while both still follow the same rule.

A fixed point is unchanged by an update; a cycle repeats. The word “chaotic” in the reading concerns deterministic dynamics that can strongly amplify uncertainty about the start. An irregular-looking line alone is not enough to establish that property.

Investigation 3

Build the next stage; account for every piece

Slide from stage 0 to stage 6. The Koch starting segment has length 1; the Sierpinski starting triangle has area 1. Predict each next count before moving the slider.

What changes at a stage?

A stage is one complete replacement of every piece in the current drawing. For Koch, stage 1 has four pieces, each one-third of the original length: total length \(4/3\). Stage 2 has sixteen pieces of length \(1/9\): total length \(16/9\).

For Sierpinski, stage 1 keeps three triangles of area \(1/4\), totaling \(3/4\). At the next stage, nine triangles of area \(1/16\) total \(9/16\). A rising piece count can accompany a falling total area because each new piece is smaller.

Investigation 4

One coastline, four reported lengths

These invented survey counts illustrate how smaller rulers can follow more boundary detail. The bars all use the same length scale.

Picture a ruler placed end to end along a winding shoreline on a map. The number of placements counts how many ruler lengths are needed. The estimate is ruler length × placements. A shorter ruler can follow more turns, so its placement count can increase enough to give a longer total.

For example, 30 placements of a 20-meter ruler give 600 meters. The bars below use kilometers, with 1,000 meters in one kilometer. Changing the selection highlights a calculation; it does not change the example’s shoreline.
