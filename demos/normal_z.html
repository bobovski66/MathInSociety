Three statistical views

The first view locates an individual measurement in a stated normal model. The second performs interval arithmetic using a supplied estimate and margin. The third repeats a sampling procedure so its coverage can be observed. Use the view that matches the question you are investigating.

Normal model and z-score

Predict a change in relative position, then adjust one quantity at a time. Start with a preset or choose a mean, standard deviation, and observation.

The table uses the requested Empirical Rule approximations, including 16%, 2.5%, and 0.15% for one tail. A normal calculator can give more precise values. The rule does not give an exact tail percentage at every possible z-score.

  1. Choose apples: mean 180 g, SD 15 g, observation 210 g. Explain the z-score and identify the upper tail beyond that point.
  2. Choose commute times: mean 35 minutes, SD 8 minutes, observation 59 minutes. Compare the observation with the three-standard-deviation boundary.

Estimate and supplied margin of error

Enter an estimate, its reported margin, and a comparison value for the same population parameter. This calculator constructs the interval; the sampling design and method must justify the supplied margin. No confidence level can be inferred from these three numbers alone.

This interval concerns the population quantity being estimated. It is not a range that must contain every individual observation. A comparison at an endpoint counts as inside; inclusion does not prove equality.

Sample size and precision

Under comparable confidence, variability, and sampling design, use \(E_{\mathrm{new}}\approx E_0\sqrt{n_0/n_{\mathrm{new}}}\). This planning calculation holds those conditions fixed; a new actual sample can also change the estimate.

Try multiplying the baseline sample by 2, 4, and 9. Explain why doubling the sample does not halve the margin. The calculation does not remove a bias in who or what was sampled.

Repeated samples and interval coverage

For this experiment, the population is normal with known mean 70 and known standard deviation 10. Each study draws an independent sample, calculates its mean, and constructs \(\bar x\pm1.96(10/\sqrt n)\), an approximately 95% known-σ interval. These assumptions let us check coverage against a known model value. The main lab supplies margins rather than asking you to calculate this multiplier.

Each horizontal segment is one study’s interval. A circle marks its sample mean. The fixed vertical reference is the population mean 70. The two line styles distinguish intervals that contain it from intervals that miss. The table gives every displayed result.

  1. Change the seed and compare coverage counts. Must a set of 50 studies have exactly 95% coverage?
  2. Keep the seed and increase n. Compare interval widths. Does the population’s individual standard deviation change?
  3. Explain why a study can miss the true mean even when its procedure is valid, and why this does not mean 95% of individual observations fall inside its interval.

Sampling is simulated with repeatable pseudorandom draws. A seed reproduces a run; it is not observed study data. In a real study the population mean is unknown, so this direct coverage check would not be available.
