Week 9 / Application

Improving routes through a network

Use routes and failure tests to recommend one new communication link. Explain what improves, what remains vulnerable, and why the objective matters.

The situation / An invented planning exercise

One new link. Which improvement matters?

A fictional county is testing a neighborhood alert network. A is the emergency coordinator, B the library, C the clinic, D the school, E the shelter, F the North Block relay, and G the community center. Each existing link is a two-way direct communication channel.

The coordinator starts with the alert at round 0. Every informed location sends to all of its neighbors during the following round. New recipients begin relaying in the next round, and no one forgets the alert. One round means one model step, not a measured number of minutes.

The county can fund one new link. Among the proposals below, your job is to recommend a link for a stated objective, support the decision with calculations, and identify information needed before using this model in practice.

Step 1 / Establish a baseline

Count what the county already has

Example · Distinguish a node from its links

The library is one node, B. Its three links are AB, BC, BD. Counting locations gives the size of the network. Counting links describes its direct contact structure.

Step 2 / Measure timing

When does each location first receive the alert?

A-to-B takes one link, so B first receives the alert in round 1. To reach D, trace A–B–D or A–C–D. Each step consumes one round under this rule.

Step 3 / Test an outage

What if D–E is unavailable?

Suppose the link between the school and shelter fails before the alert begins. All other links work. Remove only DE, keeping both locations present. Inside each region, messages can still move.

Step 4 / Compare the proposals

An extra link has to serve the objective

Assume the two genuinely new proposals, A–D and D–F, have the same installation cost. The county’s primary objective for this step is continued access to both regions if D–E fails. Other costs and failure patterns have not been measured.

Why does A–D improve delivery time? The route A–D–E–G has only three edges. Why does D–F meet the resilience goal? It supplies another way across the boundary. Equal density does not imply equal timing or equal vulnerability.

Step 5 / State the choice clearly

“Best” means best for a specified purpose

Your decision memo

Write a short recommendation to the county. Name the objective and chosen link. Use at least two pieces of evidence, including a route that survives the stated failure. Acknowledge the delivery-time tradeoff, then name one real-world unknown the county should investigate.

A useful sentence frame is: “For the goal of ___, I recommend ___. In the model, ___ because the route ___ remains available. Compared with ___, the tradeoff is ___. Before installation, we should find out ___.”

This exercise evaluates reasoning from a simplified graph. A real alert plan also needs evidence about access, staffing, transmission reliability, and whether multiple links could fail together. Your memo should distinguish the mathematical finding from the decision it informs.
