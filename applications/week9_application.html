Application — Designing a Resilient Community Alert Network

A community can send an emergency alert quickly only if the communication structure gives it reliable routes.

The situation

Lewis County is testing a simplified neighborhood alert system. Seven locations can relay a message to locations with which they have a direct communication link.

NodeLocationRole
AEmergency coordinatorStarts official alerts
BLibraryPublic information point
CClinicHealth information point
DSchoolConnects several local services
EShelterConnects to the north-side neighborhood
FNorth BlockResidential relay
GCommunity CenterResidential relay
Existing links: AB, AC, BC, BD, CD, DE, EF, EG, FG. A message crosses one link per round.

The county has funding to add one new direct communication link. Your job is to decide where that link should go and justify the decision mathematically.

Step 1 · Understand the existing network

Before improving a system, measure what it already does.

Step 2 · How fast does the alert travel?

A starts with the alert at round 0. Every informed location relays to all of its neighbors during the next round.

Hint: earliest arrival time equals shortest-path distance from A in this model.

Step 3 · Test a failure

Suppose the D–E communication link fails during a windstorm.

This is a resilience question: the original network works perfectly when every edge works, but a single failure can separate an entire region.

Step 4 · Spend the budget

The county can add one new link. Compare three proposals.

Proposal 1: A–D

Shortens some routes inside the left region, but it does not create a second connection to the right region.

Proposal 2: D–F

Creates a second cross-region route. If D–E fails, A can still reach the right region through D–F.

Proposal 3: E–F

This edge already exists, so it adds no new connectivity.

Step 5 · “Best” depends on the objective

This connects directly to optimization. Proposal D–F is attractive if the objective is resilience. But suppose the county instead cared only about making the coordinator reach the school as quickly as possible. Then A–D would reduce A-to-D distance from 2 to 1.

network design = feasible links + an objective + tradeoffs

There is no universal best network. “Best” depends on what the system is trying to protect or improve: travel time, redundancy, cost, fairness, capacity, privacy, or something else.

Decision memo

Write 3–5 sentences recommending one new link. Use at least two pieces of network evidence: degree, shortest-path distance, connected components, a bridge, or an alternate path. Then name one real-world factor this simplified graph leaves out.

A strong answer separates the mathematical claim from the policy decision: the graph can show structural consequences, but people still have to decide which consequences matter most.
