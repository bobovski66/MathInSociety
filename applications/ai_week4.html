AI Mathematics 4 — How Do We Know a Model Works?

Samples, statistics, confidence intervals, and the danger of treating a benchmark score as a universal fact.

Why this belongs in Math in Society

A model is evaluated on samples, but people often talk as though the score describes every possible prompt.

Suppose we want to know how often an AI system answers a certain kind of question correctly. The population is the full collection of prompts we care about. We cannot usually test every possible prompt, so we select a sample and compute a statistic.

The statistical lesson is the same as everywhere else: a sample result is evidence about a population, not the population itself. The prompts chosen for a benchmark matter, and an estimate should be accompanied by some description of uncertainty.

What is simplified here? Real model evaluation is more complicated than one score. Different tasks, graders, prompt styles, languages, and user populations can produce different results. A confidence interval does not repair a badly chosen sample.

1. Population or sample?

2. Build an interval.

A quality study reports an estimated population mean score of 78 with margin of error ±4.

4. Increase precision.

A benchmark using (n=100) prompts has margin of error about 6 points. About how many comparable prompts are needed to cut that margin to 3 points?

5. Be careful with close benchmark scores.
