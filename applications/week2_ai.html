Step 1 · Module 2 mathematics

Outcomes and probabilities

In Module 2, you learned to turn counts into relative frequencies and recognize a probability distribution. If 100 observations fall into four categories with counts

\[50,25,20,5,\]

then the relative frequencies are

\[0.50,0.25,0.20,0.05.\]

They are nonnegative and add to 1, so they form a probability distribution.

Step 2 · Make the AI connection

For an LLM, the categories can be possible next tokens

Suppose the current text is:

“The cat slept on the …”

A tiny model might produce:

Possible next tokenProbability
bed0.50
sofa0.25
floor0.20
moon0.05

This is the same mathematical object from Module 2: categories with probabilities totaling 1. A real LLM does not simply count these four words, but at a generation step its output can still be represented as a probability distribution over possible next tokens.

Step 3 · Worked example

Read the distribution before thinking about AI

From the table, \(P(\text{bed})=0.50\). The probability of something other than “bed” is

\[P(\text{not bed})=1-0.50=0.50.\]

If this exact distribution were used 200 independent times, the expected number of “sofa” selections would be

\[200(0.25)=50.\]

One generation can vary, while repeated generations have a long-run pattern described by the probabilities.

What to carry forward

The AI idea of this module

Module 2 supplies the second piece:

\[\text{current text}\longrightarrow\text{probabilities for possible next tokens}.\]

The model produces a whole distribution of possibilities, not just a single number.

These examples are intentionally small. Real LLMs use much larger numerical objects and many repeated layers, but the mathematical structure highlighted here is genuine.
