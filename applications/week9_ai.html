Step 1 · Module 9 mathematics

A linear program needs variables, constraints, and an objective

In Module 9, the workflow is:
  1. Define the decision variables.
  2. Translate resource limits into linear inequalities.
  3. Write an objective function.
  4. Find the feasible region.
  5. Evaluate the objective at its vertices.
The mathematics does not decide what our goal should be. It solves the optimization problem after the goal and constraints have been specified.
Step 2 · Make the AI connection

Compute and human review are limited resources

Suppose an organization uses an AI system for two kinds of jobs. Let \[ x=\text{hundreds of text jobs per day}, \] \[ y=\text{hundreds of analysis jobs per day}. \] Analysis jobs use more computing capacity, while text jobs use more staff-review time. This is hypothetical, but it creates exactly the same kind of feasible-region problem you have been solving.

Keep the scale in perspective: this page uses tiny numbers so you can see the mathematics. Real language models use many-dimensional vectors, very large vocabularies, and many repeated layers. The simplified calculation is a model of one mathematical structure, not a miniature copy of the whole system.

Step 3 · Worked example

Translate the story into a linear program

Suppose the daily limits are \[ x+2y\le10 \qquad\text{(compute capacity)} \] and \[ 2x+y\le14 \qquad\text{(staff-review capacity)}, \] with \[ x\ge0,\qquad y\ge0. \] Suppose the organization assigns a benefit score \[ B=3x+5y. \] The point \((2,3)\) is feasible because \[ 2+2(3)=8\le10 \] and \[ 2(2)+3=7\le14. \] Its objective value is \[ B=3(2)+5(3)=21. \] We would compare this with the objective values at the other vertices.

What to carry forward

The AI idea of this module

Linear programming can optimize a stated AI resource-allocation goal, but it cannot tell us whether the objective itself is wise, fair, or socially desirable. Mathematics clarifies the consequences of a model; people remain responsible for choosing the model.
