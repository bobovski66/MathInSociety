Module 4: AI Application — Measuring an AI System with Samples

Why a model benchmark is an inference problem, not a magic score.

Why this page exists

Mathematics gives us a way into AI

When people say an AI system is “85% accurate,” the number usually comes from a finite collection of test questions. That means the reported accuracy is a sample statistic. The larger population of possible questions is much bigger.

Important: the examples on this page are intentionally tiny models. Real LLMs use huge vectors, many layers, and billions of learned numerical parameters. We simplify the scale while keeping the mathematical structure recognizable.

Connection to this module

The mathematics you already know

Module 4 gives us exactly the tools needed to interpret benchmark results: samples, sampling variation, margins of error, and confidence intervals.

AI vocabulary

Four ideas to carry with you

benchmarkA collection of test tasks used to measure a model.
sample accuracyThe fraction of tested items answered correctly.
population of promptsThe broader collection of questions we would like the test to represent.
margin of errorA measure of uncertainty around a sample estimate.
Worked example

Follow the numbers

Suppose an AI answers 82 of 100 sampled questions correctly. Its sample accuracy is \[ \hat p=\frac{82}{100}=0.82=82\%. \] If a simplified classroom calculation gives a margin of error of about 8 percentage points, we would report an interval \[ 82\%\pm8\%= [74\%,90\%]. \] The point is not that the model's “true intelligence” is somewhere in this interval. The interval concerns performance on a particular population of tasks under a particular testing method.

What to remember

AI idea of the week

AI evaluation is statistics. Scores depend on what was sampled, how it was scored, and what population of tasks we are trying to generalize to. A benchmark number should always be read together with its design.

You do not need to know every engineering detail of an LLM to reason mathematically about what it is doing. We will keep adding one mathematical layer at a time.
