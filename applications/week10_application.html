Week 10 / Application

Comparing population forecasts

Two starting estimates differ by ten fish. Use the same deterministic rule to discover when their model forecasts become substantially different.

The situation

A difference of ten fish

Our model follows an estimated fish population one breeding season at a time. Here we measure an estimated number of fish. One update means one breeding season, also called a generation in this model. Our question is how long two nearby starting estimates produce similar predictions.

A field team is studying a lake population using a deliberately simplified model. They choose a reference population of \(K=100{,}000\) fish and write the current population as a fraction \(x=N/K\).

Why change a fish count into a fraction?

The reference population makes a convenient common scale. Dividing by 100,000 converts fish into a fraction; multiplying by 100,000 converts the fraction back into fish. This is the same relationship between a part and a whole used for relative frequencies in Week 2.

Fish estimate Fraction of 100,000 Percentage
20,000 0.20 20%
50,000 0.50 50%
100,000 1.00 100%

The letters \(x_n\) and \(y_n\) name two forecast sequences. They describe alternative estimates of the same lake, not two different lakes. The subscript \(n\) counts breeding seasons from the initial survey.

\(x_{n+1}=3.9x_n(1-x_n),\qquad N_n=100{,}000x_n.\)

Read the update as “next fraction = 3.9 × current fraction × (1 − current fraction).” The setting 3.9 is held fixed. The limiting factor \(1-x_n\) changes with the population, so the whole multiplier changes from season to season. To obtain a second generation, insert the first result into the formula.

The survey is consistent with either \(x_0=0.4000\) or \(y_0=0.4001\). Those are nearby estimates of the same starting population. The model’s parameter remains 3.9 in both calculations.

What this model lets us investigate

This is a mathematical experiment about measurement uncertainty. The reference population gives the fractions a scale; it does not establish a real lake’s ecological carrying capacity. No field data have been fitted here.

Example · Convert the model’s units

A state of 0.25 represents \(0.25(100{,}000)=25{,}000\) fish. A difference of 0.01 represents 1,000 fish. The phrase “1% of the reference population” means this fixed difference, not 1% of whichever population happens to occur later.

Part 3 / Forecast horizons

When does the gap matter?

A tolerance says how much disagreement is acceptable for a particular question. A threshold is the cutoff used to check it. In this investigation, we use fixed cutoffs measured against the reference population.

Fractional gap Gap as percentage of the reference Difference in fish
0.01 1% 1,000
0.10 10% 10,000

How to find the first crossing

Read the absolute-gap column from generation 0 downward. For the 0.01 question, ignore rows at or below 0.01 and stop at the first row above it. Repeat separately for the 0.10 question. The generations need not be the same.

At the first row above 0.10, multiply that row’s fractional gap by 100,000 and round to the nearest fish. This asks for the size of the difference at that generation, rather than either forecast’s total population.

The two thresholds below are decision tolerances for this experiment. A first crossing remains the first crossing even if the gap later shrinks again.

Use the result carefully

This is called sensitivity to initial conditions: the calculated future can depend strongly on a small difference in the starting information. The model is still deterministic, meaning the same exact input always has the same next result. We changed the starting estimate, not the formula.

The first gap above 1,000 fish occurs sooner than the first gap above 10,000 fish. That is why a useful forecast horizon must name a tolerance. Monitoring again can reduce current uncertainty and restart the comparison, although it cannot make an incomplete model exact.

Even after exact long-range trajectories become unreliable, questions about ranges, typical behavior, or short-term responses can remain useful. The right question depends on the decision.
