Week 10 / Application

How long can a prediction guide a decision?

A conservation team is comparing two forecasts made from almost identical starting measurements. The question is not which formula to invent; it is how much confidence to place in a forecast before collecting new observations.

The situation

Two close measurements of a lake population

A lake conservation team estimates a fish population. It uses a fraction of a reference population, rather than an exact head count. Two surveys produce starting fractions 0.4000 and 0.4001. The team tests a simplified logistic model, \(x_{n+1}=3.9x_n(1-x_n)\), to see how these small differences might affect its projected values.

The fraction \(x\) is a mathematical state between zero and one. Each update represents one hypothetical breeding season. The logistic equation has not been calibrated to this lake. Its role here is to show what can happen when a forecast depends sensitively on an uncertain starting state.

QuantitySurvey ASurvey B
Starting fraction0.40000.4001
Parameter \(r\)3.93.9
Update ruleMultiply the current fraction by \(3.9(1-\text{current fraction})\)

The team chooses a difference of 0.1 between predicted fractions as an illustration of a decision threshold. This is a teaching threshold, not an established biological safety standard.

The first prediction

Use the rule for Survey A first. Substitute 0.4000 into both appearances of the current fraction. Then do the same for Survey B.

2 · Follow the consequences

Look beyond the first update

Open the Week 10 Explorer, choose the logistic map, and enter the same two starting values with \(r=3.9\). Compare the two trajectories over 40 updates.

Now change the parameter to \(r=2.8\), keeping the two starting values. Compare the resulting behavior. What does that tell us about the role of the rule's parameter?

3 · Make a recommendation

What would the field team need next?

These experiments show that the mathematical forecast can be sensitive. They do not prove that the real lake follows the logistic map, or that two actual surveys are equally reliable. A sensible monitoring plan would need observations, a tested model, and a clear decision criterion.
