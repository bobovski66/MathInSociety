1. Return to recursion

Think back to Week 1. A recursive rule specifies how one value determines the next. A starting value plus an update rule creates a sequence. The rule itself may be short, but its repeated behavior may be difficult to anticipate.

Worked example. With \(a_{n+1}=3a_n\) and \(a_0=1\), successive values are \(1,3,9,27,81,\ldots\). Starting from \(a_0=1.01\), the values become \(1.01,3.03,9.09,27.27,81.81,\ldots\). The absolute difference grows, but the percentage difference remains about 1%. This is an important reminder that different measures of difference can tell different stories.

In later examples, even the relative difference between two nearby starting values may grow dramatically. That possibility is one source of difficulty in long-term prediction.

2. The Collatz conjecture: simple rules, difficult questions

The Collatz recursion applies one of two rules to a positive integer: if the current number is even, divide it by two; if it is odd, multiply by three and add one.

\[a_{n+1}=\begin{cases}a_n/2,&a_n\text{ even},\\3a_n+1,&a_n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}\]

Worked example. Start at 6. It is even, so go to 3. Then 3 is odd, so \(3(3)+1=10\). Continue: \(6,3,10,5,16,8,4,2,1\). After reaching 1, the sequence cycles through \(1,4,2,1,\ldots\).

The Collatz conjecture says every positive integer eventually reaches 1 under this rule. It has been tested for enormously many starting values but remains unproved. Checking many examples provides evidence, not a proof covering every possible integer.

Try it. Follow the Collatz rule starting from 7 until you first reach 1. What makes an odd step different from an even step?

3. Self-similarity and fractal geometry

Some geometric shapes display similar patterns at different scales. This property is called self-similarity. A small part of the shape resembles the whole, sometimes exactly and sometimes only approximately.

For example, the Koch curve starts with a straight segment. Divide it into three equal parts, remove the middle third, and replace that missing piece with two sides of an outward-pointing equilateral triangle. Repeat the same construction on every new segment.

After each stage, the number of segments is multiplied by four, while each segment is one-third as long. The total length is multiplied by \(4/3\) at every stage.

Worked example. If the original segment has length 1, the first stage has total length \(4/3\). The second stage has \((4/3)^2=16/9\). Repeated scaling makes the boundary longer and longer, even though the construction remains within a bounded region.

Fractal geometry studies patterns that often have rich structure at many magnifications. Natural examples include coastlines, branching trees, and certain cloud or river patterns, although natural objects are only approximately fractal across a limited range of scales.

4. The logistic map: feedback from growth and limits

A population cannot grow without limit when resources are finite. The logistic map is a simple rule combining growth with a restriction:

\[x_{n+1}=r x_n(1-x_n).\]

Here \(x_n\) is a normalized population or activity level between 0 and 1, and \(r\) controls the strength of the update. The factor \(x_n\) encourages growth when a population exists; the factor \(1-x_n\) reduces growth as the population approaches its upper bound.

Worked example. Let \(r=2\) and \(x_0=0.2\). Then \(x_1=2(0.2)(0.8)=0.32\). Next, \(x_2=2(0.32)(0.68)=0.4352\). Repeating the rule moves the values toward \(0.5\).

At other values of \(r\), the long-term behavior can settle into repeating cycles or become chaotic. We should not assume that a rule is chaotic merely because it is nonlinear.

Connection to Week 5. The term \(1-x_n\) provides a form of limiting feedback. Local growth and limiting effects interact to determine what happens next.

5. Sensitivity and the limits of prediction

Some dynamical systems are sensitive to initial conditions: starting from two values that differ only slightly, their later trajectories may become very different. In a chaotic system, long-term prediction can be difficult even when the rule is known exactly.

This does not mean the system has no rule. The distinction is between knowing the rule and knowing the starting conditions with unlimited precision. Small measurement uncertainty can eventually matter a great deal.

Everyday analogy. A forecast begins with imperfect measurements of the atmosphere. Complex dynamics can magnify the effects of those small uncertainties, so a long-range forecast is less reliable than a short-range one. This is part of why forecasters use multiple scenarios instead of pretending to know one exact future.

Some systems are predictable, some stabilize, and some become difficult to forecast. We discover which behavior occurs by studying the update rule together with its parameters and starting conditions.

6. Fractals, boundaries, and scale

The coastline paradox illustrates why measurement sometimes depends on scale. Measuring a jagged coastline with a very large ruler skips small bays and inlets. A smaller ruler follows more of the irregular boundary and may report a longer length.

For a smooth curve, better measurements approach a well-defined length. For an idealized fractal boundary, the measured length may continue to grow as the ruler gets smaller. This is not an error in arithmetic: it reflects the geometry of the object and the method of measurement.

Fractals are useful when ordinary shapes—circles, rectangles, straight lines—do not adequately describe repeated detail. They remind us to specify not only what is measured, but also how finely it is observed.

Think it through. Why might two maps give different coastline lengths even when both are carefully measured?

7. The course's recurring question: what can we know?

Across ten weeks, we have learned several ways mathematics connects observations with the world. Number systems let us represent quantities. Probability and statistics describe variation. Feedback systems explain how observations influence new actions. Optimization and networks describe choices and connections. Recursion and fractals reveal the behavior of repeated rules.

There is a common lesson: a model is useful when we understand its assumptions and its purpose. A prediction may be uncertain because the measurements are noisy, because the model leaves out important information, or because the system magnifies small differences.

Final thought. Mathematics does more than produce answers. It helps us distinguish what a pattern tells us from what we still need to investigate.
