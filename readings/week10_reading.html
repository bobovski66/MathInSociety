Week 10 / Reading

Complexity, Chaos, and Fractals

A short mathematical rule can be easy to follow and still lead somewhere surprising. We will begin with calculations, notice what happens, and then give the results their mathematical names.

CollatzCompare neighboring starts: why do they need such different amounts of work? The logistic mapCompare two nearly identical measurements over many updates. Cantor set and Sierpiński triangleRemove material, then count pieces and measure what is left.
Begin with something you can calculate

Two neighboring numbers

You've already spent this course learning how to work with rules: savings balances change from month to month, decisions lead to more decisions, and a sequence is built from the number before it. A rule tells us how to get the next result. But how much does it tell us about what will happen after many steps? Let's investigate rather than guess.

Choose a positive whole number. If it is even, divide by 2. If it is odd, multiply by 3 and add 1. Keep applying the rule to each new number. Start at 8:

8 → 4 → 2 → 1

That took three updates. Count the arrows rather than the numbers written down. Now start at 9. It's only one larger than 8. Before calculating, predict whether it will take about the same number of updates. Then work through the sequence until it first reaches 1.

Compare your calculation. How many updates did you need? Did the numbers ever climb above 9?

Open the completed trajectory

9 → 28 → 14 → 7 → 22 → 11 → 34 → 17 → 52 → 26 → 13 → 40 → 20 → 10 → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1

This route takes 19 updates, and its largest value is 52.

Why should two nearby starting numbers give such different experiences? That's our first puzzle. We'll meet two more: one about trusting a forecast, and one about making a geometric pattern by removing pieces. In each case, doing some elementary mathematics will help us discover what the problem actually is.

Investigation 1 · Computation

How much work will the rule take?

The rule we just used is called the Collatz rule. Here's a compact way to write exactly the same two instructions:

\[T(n)=\begin{cases}n/2,&n\text{ is even},\\3n+1,&n\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}\]

Let's compare two more neighbors: 10 and 11. Start each separately and write its trajectory, the sequence of values produced. As you work, keep track of two things: the number of arrows before the first 1, and the highest number you encounter.

The first is the stopping time, written \(\tau(n)\), when the sequence reaches 1; the second is the peak. These measure different things. One tells us how many times we performed an operation, and the other tells us how large the intermediate numbers became.

Now imagine programming a computer to perform the updates. Someone types in 100. The computer certainly knows the next instruction: 100 is even, so divide by 2. But how many updates will the whole calculation take? Nothing about the number 100 immediately tells us that.

This is our first encounter with the idea of computational work. A short rule doesn't necessarily mean a short calculation. Nearby starting values don't have to require similar numbers of steps, either. Notice that this is a statement about the examples we've calculated; we're not claiming to have found a universal pattern in stopping times.

Computational complexity studies the resources a computation needs—such as steps, running time, or memory—and how they depend on its input. Counting the Collatz updates for particular starting values is an elementary way to notice that workload matters. It does not classify the formal complexity of the Collatz problem.

One bigger question remains. We have seen several starting numbers reach 1. Would every positive whole number eventually reach it? The Collatz conjecture says yes, but mathematicians have not proved it. Checking a million numbers would tell us what happened for those million numbers. A proof about all positive integers would need something more.

What is the evidence? From the four rows above, what can we say for certain? Could we use them alone to tell how many steps the number 100 will take—or to prove the conjecture?

Think back to Week 9

Sometimes we can predict the work ahead of time

Consider a different task: find one name among 32 names. If the names are in no particular order, you may need to check all 32 in the worst case. But suppose the list is alphabetical. You can check the middle, throw out half the possibilities, and repeat.

How quickly does the list shrink? You have at most 16 possibilities after the first comparison, then 8, then 4, then 2, then 1. That's just five comparisons, provided the list has the sorted structure needed for this method. This connects directly to the binary decision trees from last week.

Here we can predict the number of decisions without carrying them out. Collatz has a simple next-step rule too, but we can't read its stopping time so easily from the starting number. That is the distinction we want to notice—not that one example looks more complicated than the other.

So the first question of the week was: How much computation does a rule require? Our next question is different: How reliable is a result that a rule predicts?

Investigation 2 · Prediction

What if our starting measurement isn't exact?

Suppose a conservation team is estimating the number of fish in a lake. They don't count every fish. Instead they use a measurement expressed as a fraction between 0 and 1. One survey gives 0.4000 and another gives 0.4001. Those numbers are very close, and both might be reasonable estimates of the lake's present state.

The team wants to see what would happen if each estimate were used in the same mathematical forecasting rule. Would you expect the two predictions to stay close? Before answering, let's actually calculate.

The logistic map updates a number according to

\[x_{n+1}=r x_n(1-x_n).\]

Here \(x_n\) is the current value, and \(x_{n+1}\) is the new value. The parameter \(r\) controls the update; we'll use \(r=3.9\). This is a simplified mathematical model, not a formula that has been tested and calibrated for this particular lake.

For the first estimate, the first update is

\(x_1=3.9(0.4000)(1-0.4000)=3.9(0.4000)(0.6000)=0.936000.\)

Now try the same calculation with 0.4001. Be careful to subtract the new input from 1. You should get approximately 0.936078. The first-step predictions are still nearly identical.

What about after ten or twenty updates? Doing all of that arithmetic by hand would take a while, so we'll let a computer repeat the very same calculation. Make a prediction first: will the small gap remain small, increase steadily, or behave in a less predictable way?

Start by comparing step 5: the two predictions are still quite close. But at step 15 the first predicts roughly 0.933 and the other roughly 0.637. Their difference is nearly 0.296, even though the original measurements differed by only 0.0001.

Try subtracting the two entries at steps 10 and 20 as well. You'll discover something important: by step 20 the gap has actually become smaller than at step 15. The two trajectories don't simply move apart by a larger amount at every step. They can move closer and farther apart again.

A decision for the conservation team. If these were projections for a real lake, would you trust a long-term prediction based on one imperfect survey? What additional information would you want before making a decision?

One possible interpretation

It would help to take new measurements and test whether the model describes the lake accurately. Even a perfectly applied formula cannot remove uncertainty in its starting measurement, or compensate for a model that doesn't describe the real situation.

Sensitive dependence on initial conditions occurs when initially close states can become noticeably separated under repeated updates. This is a central feature of chaos. The logistic map with \(r=3.9\) is in a familiar chaotic regime. The equation does not make random choices; the difficulty is forecasting far into the future when we cannot measure the start with unlimited precision. One pair of separating trajectories alone would not prove chaos for an arbitrary rule.

What if we change \(r\) instead? At \(r=2.8\), the sequence typically settles toward one level; at \(r=3.2\), it approaches two alternating values; and at \(r=3.9\), it can behave irregularly for long periods. The Week 10 Explorer lets you compare them. Watch the behavior after many updates, not just the first two.

Our first investigation asked about how long a calculation might take. Our second has asked whether an exact rule guarantees an accurate long-term prediction. Now let's use a repeated rule to build a picture instead.

Investigation 3 · Geometry

How can taking pieces away leave more pieces behind?

Imagine a strip of paper one meter long. Mark it into three equal parts and remove the open middle third. You're left with two shorter strips, each one third of a meter long. Now remove the middle third of each of those strips. Repeat the same instruction every time.

This is the middle-thirds Cantor construction. You could carry out the first couple of steps with real paper. After that the pieces become difficult to cut, but the mathematical rule can keep going.

We can ask two questions about what remains. First, how many separate pieces are there? We start with 1, then have 2, then 4, then 8. Second, how much total length is left? At stage 1, two strips of length \(1/3\) add up to \(2/3\). At stage 2, four strips of length \(1/9\) add up to \(4/9\).

So the number of pieces is getting bigger while their combined length is getting smaller. This isn't a contradiction: counting pieces and measuring length are different measurements.

There are \(2^5=32\) intervals, each \(1/3^5=1/243\) long. Together they have length \(32/243\). The number of pieces doubles at each stage, while total length is multiplied by \(2/3\).

If you look closely at any remaining piece, the same left-and-right pattern begins again. This is self-similarity: parts of a geometric object resemble smaller copies of the whole. The ideal Cantor set is what remains if we continue the removals indefinitely. Its total length tends to zero, even though many points survive.

A connection to Week 9 · Optional exploration

Can we give a point an address?

In Week 9, we used strings of zeros and ones to describe paths through a decision tree. The Cantor construction gives those paths a location on a number line. At every stage, a surviving interval has a left third and a right third. Let's call the choices L and R.

If our address begins with R, we're in the interval \([2/3,1]\). Suppose the next letter is L. That means take the left surviving third of the interval we've already selected. Our address is now RL, and the point lies in \([2/3,7/9]\).

Choose R one more time. The address RLR puts us inside \([20/27,21/27]\). Notice how we've located a point more precisely each time, without naming a decimal number.

AddressPossible locationInterval length
R\([2/3,1]\)\(1/3\)
RL\([2/3,7/9]\)\(1/9\)
RLR\([20/27,21/27]\)\(1/27\)

Imagine continuing the address forever. The intervals keep getting smaller—\(1/3,1/9,1/27,\ldots\)—and each one is contained inside the previous one. Could two different points remain in every interval? No: eventually the intervals are shorter than any fixed positive distance between two points. The nested closed intervals determine exactly one point in the Cantor set.

Here's a neat consequence. If two points have the same first four L/R choices, both lie inside one interval of length \(1/81\). What is the greatest distance they could be apart?

See the answer

At most \(1/81\). Agreement in the first four choices gives us a guarantee about geometric closeness.

That's a new way to think about a real number: an infinite sequence of choices can pick out one exact point. A finite address gives only an approximate location—but that can be enough for a map, a digital image, or a computer calculation. We don't need infinite-series formulas to grasp the idea. Our Week 9 decision tree has become a geometric addressing system.

Connection to our class example

The Sierpiński triangle: the same question in two dimensions

We started the Cantor construction with a strip. What if we start with a filled triangle? Divide it into four equally sized smaller triangles, remove the middle one, and repeat on each surviving triangle. This is how we build the Sierpiński triangle.

Before checking the table, imagine one stage: three little triangles survive. At the next stage, each of those produces three more. So the count goes from 3 to 9. But does the total filled area increase?

Each small triangle has half the side length and one quarter of the area of its parent. Since we keep three of the four pieces, we keep \(3/4\) of the filled area at each stage.

As with Cantor, the number of pieces grows while their combined size decreases. Cantor uses length; Sierpiński uses area. Both ideal constructions have detail at arbitrarily small scales and are examples of fractals. You can explore more stages in the lecture atlas.

Optional extension · Beyond the exercises

Between the familiar dimensions

A line segment is one-dimensional and a filled square is two-dimensional. Fractals suggest a more subtle question: how does the number of smaller copies compare with how much each copy is reduced?

Cut a line into two half-size pieces and you can rebuild the whole line. Four half-size squares make a full square. The Sierpiński triangle consists of three half-size copies, while the Cantor set consists of two third-size copies. Those aren't the same scaling patterns.

ObjectCopiesSize of each copy
Line segment21/2
Cantor set21/3
Sierpiński triangle31/2
Filled square41/2

For certain exactly self-similar shapes, mathematicians define a similarity dimension using \(N=s^D\), where \(N\) copies of size \(1/s\) reproduce the whole. It leads to these two different values:

\[D_{\text{Cantor}}=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}\approx0.631,\qquad D_{\text{Sierpiński}}=\frac{\log 3}{\log 2}\approx1.585.\]

These logarithms are an optional mathematical extension; you do not need to calculate them for this week's work. The important thing for now is that we can compare shapes by asking how many copies appear and how much smaller they are. For these two ideal constructions, the values also give their Hausdorff dimensions. The University of Waterloo explanation explores the idea further.

Real coastlines, clouds, and branching plants can show scale-dependent structure, but they aren't exact Sierpiński triangles or Cantor sets. Looking intricate by itself is not a mathematical definition of a fractal.

Bring it together

What can a simple rule actually tell us?

Think back to the first small calculation with 8 and 9. We started by doing arithmetic and comparing the results. We did the same with two nearly identical forecasts, and then with a strip of paper. Only after noticing something surprising did we give the behavior its mathematical name.

ExampleWhat we calculatedWhat we needed to think about
CollatzValues, updates, and peaksCan we anticipate the workload? Will all starting numbers reach 1?
Logistic mapTwo forecasts and their differencesCan we trust a long-term prediction from an uncertain measurement?
Cantor setNumbers of pieces and total lengthsWhat does continuing the construction forever mean?
Sierpiński triangleNumbers of triangles and filled areaHow can a pattern reproduce itself at smaller scales?

All of these examples use recursion: the current step provides the information for the next one. But a recursive rule isn't automatically chaotic or fractal, and a short instruction isn't automatically a short computation. We have to ask the right question about each situation.

Those questions matter beyond mathematics class. A computer search consumes time and memory. A prediction depends on its measurements and its model. A map or digital image gives an approximate location at a particular resolution. Thinking mathematically means asking what our calculation tells us—and what it doesn't.

The Week 10 Explorer lets you check the patterns visually. In the guided laboratory, you'll do the mathematics and explain what you found; in the discussion, you'll examine a claim that goes further than the available evidence.

One last thought: a mathematical rule can be simple enough to fit on one line and still lead to questions that mathematicians are working to understand. That's one reason the elementary examples we've studied can open onto much deeper mathematics.
