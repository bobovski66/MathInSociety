Week 9 · Complexity and Computation

Numbers, codes, and computation

A computer stores information using two possible states. How can two symbols represent large numbers, support arithmetic, and eventually tell a machine what to do?

Why would we count in another base?

Think of a row of switches. Each switch can be off or on. One switch describes two possibilities. Two switches describe four combinations: off–off, off–on, on–off, and on–on. Three switches describe eight. With ten switches, there are already 1,024 possible patterns.

A digital device can use those patterns to represent quantities, locations in memory, or instructions. To make that possible, we need a number system that works with two symbols. That is binary. We will also learn octal, which uses eight digits and gives us a compact way to write groups of binary digits.

There is nothing mysterious about the numbers themselves. Thirteen objects are still thirteen objects whether we write 13 in decimal, 1101 in binary, or 15 in octal. The symbols change; the quantity does not. Even ordinary arithmetic—carrying, borrowing, addition, subtraction—works the same way once we know how much each place is worth.

Later in the week, the binary digits will take on another role. A sequence such as 101 can describe a route through a decision tree, and that tree can serve as a simple program. First, though, we need to learn to read and calculate with the digits.

1. What a number's position means

Look at the decimal number 347. Its digits tell us about three kinds of groups: 3 hundreds, 4 tens, and 7 ones. In other words,

347 = 3 × 100 + 4 × 10 + 7 × 1.

The digit 3 does not always mean three ones. Here, its position makes it worth three hundreds. The base tells us how quickly the place values grow. Decimal has base ten, so each step to the left is worth ten times as much.

Carrying also comes from the base. When we add 9 + 1 in decimal, ten ones become one ten, so we write 10. In another base, we will regroup after a different number of units, but we will still be exchanging a full group of smaller units for one larger unit.

A little subscript tells us which base a written number uses. For instance, 102 is worth two in decimal, while 108 is worth eight. Without a subscript, we generally assume decimal.

Check your reasoning

Both use one of the next larger place and zero ones. In binary that next place is worth 2; in octal it is worth 8.

2. Reading and writing binary numbers

A binary digit is called a bit. Its value is either 0 or 1. From the rightmost place, the values double each time we move left: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and so on. A bit equal to 1 includes that place value; a bit equal to 0 leaves it out.

Place value32168421
Binary digits101101

Reading binary: 1011012

Use the place values wherever the digit is 1:

32 + 8 + 4 + 1 = 45.

Thus 1011012 = 4510. Notice that 101101 is a string of bits; it is not meant to be read as the decimal number one hundred one thousand one hundred one.

Conversion in the other direction asks us to choose which powers of two will add up to a decimal number. For 29, the largest place we can use is 16. That leaves 13. We can use 8 (leaving 5), then 4 (leaving 1), then 1. The 2's place is skipped.

3. Binary arithmetic: carrying and borrowing

We already know how to add in columns. Binary addition follows the same procedure, starting at the right. The difference is that a place can hold only 0 or 1. When a column contains two ones, it has a value of two, which becomes 102: we write 0 and carry 1 into the next place.

4. Counting in eights

Binary is convenient for two-state devices, but a long sequence of bits can be hard for people to read. Octal gives us a more compact notation. Octal is base eight, so it uses the digits 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7. There is no digit 8 in octal. After 7, the next number is written 108.

The places in octal are worth 1, 8, 64, 512, and so on. The number 1578 therefore means:

1 × 64 + 5 × 8 + 7 × 1 = 11110.

Convert decimal 83 to octal

Find the largest power of eight no greater than 83. That's 64. We have 19 left, which contains two groups of eight and three ones. Therefore

83 = 1 × 64 + 2 × 8 + 3 × 1.

Reading the number of groups gives 8310 = 1238.

We can also divide repeatedly by eight: 83 ÷ 8 gives quotient 10 remainder 3; 10 ÷ 8 gives quotient 1 remainder 2; 1 ÷ 8 gives quotient 0 remainder 1. Reading the remainders upward again gives 1238.

The method is exactly the method we used for binary, with eight in place of two. Every positional base uses powers of its base as place values.

Work it out. Read 2478 as a decimal quantity, then convert 9710 to octal.

Check your reasoning

2478 = 2 × 64 + 4 × 8 + 7 = 167. And 97 = 64 + 4 × 8 + 1, so 9710 = 1418.

5. Octal addition and subtraction

When adding octal numbers, we carry whenever a column totals eight or more. For example, 7 + 1 in the ones column equals eight, so we write 0 and carry 1 into the eights column. A column totaling 13 in ordinary counting means one group of eight and five left over: write 5 and carry 1.

6. Why octal and binary fit together

We now have two number systems with different-looking place values. But octal has a special relationship to binary: 8 = 2 × 2 × 2. Three binary places can represent exactly eight patterns, numbered from zero through seven. One octal digit can stand for those three bits.

Octal digit01234567
Three binary bits000001010011100101110111

To change a binary number to octal, group its bits in sets of three starting at the right. Add leading zeros on the left if necessary; they do not change the quantity. Each group then becomes one octal digit.

Convert 1101012 to octal

Split the bits into 110 | 101. The first group is six, and the second is five. Therefore

1101012 = 658.

Both represent 53 in decimal (32 + 16 + 4 + 1 = 53, or 6 × 8 + 5 = 53).

Convert 7258 to binary

Replace each octal digit with exactly three binary bits: 7 → 111, 2 → 010, 5 → 101. Joining those groups gives

7258 = 1110101012.

The zeros inside each three-bit group matter. A group of 010 cannot be shortened to 10 in the middle of a code.

This makes octal useful when we want to read or record long binary patterns more economically. It has also been used in computing to summarize groups of three on/off settings, such as certain file permissions. The underlying bits have not changed; we have chosen a shorter way to display them.

Work it out. Convert 10110102 to octal by grouping from the right, and convert 348 to binary.

Check your reasoning

Pad the first number on the left: 001 | 011 | 010, giving 1328. The second becomes 011 | 100, or 111002 after omitting the unnecessary leading zero.

7. Multiplication uses the same grouping

Multiplication works in any base as repeated addition. In binary, multiplying by a 0 contributes nothing, while multiplying by a 1 copies the number. A digit in the twos place shifts the copied number one position to the left.

The first 1 gives 101. The next 1 represents two, so its partial product is 1010. Their sum is 11112, or 15 in decimal. That agrees with 5 × 3 = 15.

In octal, the ordinary multiplication facts still give the amount, but we write the result using octal digits. For example, 68 × 38 is eighteen in decimal, written 228 (two eights and two ones). Likewise, 128 × 38 = 368.

Multiplication is an extension here. Be sure you can convert, add, and subtract before spending much time on multi-column products.

8. From digits to addresses

Consider 101 again. As a binary number, it means five. But if a person is following a branching menu, those digits can mean three choices: right, left, right. The string is the same; its meaning depends on how we decide to use it.

Imagine a library's automated phone system. Each question offers two responses, numbered 0 and 1. A sequence of answers locates a particular service. We can draw every possible sequence as a branching tree.

After two choices there are four addresses: 00, 01, 10, and 11.

A two-digit address records two choices. For example, 01 means go left, then right. If another choice is made, the address becomes 010 or 011. We call 01 a prefix of either longer address.

One choice creates two addresses, two choices create four, and three choices create eight. Each new choice doubles the number of possible routes. After n choices there are 2n addresses, because each position can be 0 or 1.

Work it out. How many five-digit binary addresses are possible? How many begin with 10?

Check your reasoning

There are 25 = 32 five-digit addresses. After the fixed beginning 10, three choices remain, giving 23 = 8 possible addresses.

9. A tree becomes a small program

A community center receives calls about pantry hours, bus times, regular rides, and urgent rides. It could make every caller answer two questions, which gives four equally short addresses. Another approach is to place some services nearer the beginning of the tree.

ServiceCodeQuestions answered
Pantry hours01
Bus times102
Regular ride help1103
Urgent ride help1113

The digits are instructions: follow the indicated branch, then stop when you arrive at a service. For code 110, the system follows right, right, left and reaches regular ride help after three questions.

A stopping code cannot be the beginning of another stopping code. Otherwise, the system would not know whether to stop or wait for another digit. This condition is called prefix-free. For example, if 1 were itself a complete service code, none of 10, 110, or 111 could be reached.

Why might we put some services closer to the beginning? Imagine the center gets eight calls for pantry hours, four for bus times, two for regular rides, and two for urgent rides. With these codes, callers answer

8 × 1 + 4 × 2 + 2 × 3 + 2 × 3 = 28 questions altogether.

If all sixteen callers answered exactly two questions, they would answer 32 questions altogether. The branching menu saves four questions for this mix of calls.

This calculation gives one measure of efficiency, but it does not settle which design is best. A person seeking urgent help may need a shorter route even if that makes the total number of questions larger. Mathematical models help us compare choices; the priorities still have to come from the people using the system.

Work it out. A menu now receives six pantry calls, five bus calls, three regular-ride calls, and two urgent-ride calls. How many questions does the branching menu ask? How many would a two-question menu ask?

Check your reasoning

The branching menu asks 6(1) + 5(2) + 3(3) + 2(3) = 31 questions. With sixteen callers, a fixed two-question menu asks 32. The branching menu saves one question in total.

10. What happens when the choices keep going?

The help-line program has four stopping points, so every caller is finished after at most three questions. A binary tree, though, can continue to another level after any address. Each possible address has two extensions, one ending in 0 and the other in 1.

A short rule can limit the available routes. Suppose a code must never contain two consecutive ones, 11. At length two, the allowed strings are 00, 01, and 10. At length three, there are five: 000, 001, 010, 100, and 101. The number of possible strings changes when we change the rule.

Notice that the rule says nothing about what the digits mean. It only tells us which strings are allowed. A code system or program can impose restrictions on patterns even when it uses the very same bits to store those patterns.

Further question: a tree without a final level

In an infinite binary tree, any finite address can be extended by another digit. A never-ending path would be an infinite sequence of zeros and ones, while a finite address records only the choices made so far. The phone menu uses a finite part of this larger tree and includes instructions that tell a caller when to stop.

Other programs can repeat operations many times. The number of available paths and the number of steps an instruction takes are different questions. That distinction leads toward the study of computational complexity.

Work it out. Give two four-digit strings that avoid 11 and one that violates the rule. If an allowed address ends in 1, what must its next digit be?

Check your reasoning

For example, 0101 and 1001 are allowed; 0110 is forbidden. A string ending in 1 can only be extended by 0 without creating 11.

What have we learned about computation?

Counting in a different base does not change a quantity. It changes how we represent it. The same place-value and regrouping principles allow us to convert and calculate in binary and octal. When we use binary strings as addresses, each digit becomes a choice instead of a number to be added. A decision tree adds stopping instructions, turning those choices into a small program.
