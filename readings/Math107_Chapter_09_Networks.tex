\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section*{Start here: connections change what a community can do}


A county has seven places that can relay a public alert: a coordinator, library, clinic, school, shelter, residential block, and community center. The county knows which places can contact one another directly. Will an alert reach everyone? How long could it take? What happens if one communication link fails?



These questions concern \emph{relationships}. Counting the seven locations gives part of the story. Recording how they connect gives the rest. A \emph{network} is a mathematical model that keeps those connections visible.



You already have the tools to begin. From Weeks 1--4, we will use counting, fractions, percentages, and averages. From Weeks 5--6, we will use a rule repeatedly, keeping track of the current state. From Weeks 7--8, we will ask what we are trying to improve and which choices are allowed. No previous study of networks is assumed.



\Needspace{5\baselineskip}\subsection*{By the end, you can\ldots{}}

\begin{itemize}
\item Translate a real situation into nodes and links.
\item Count direct neighbors and measure the network's density.
\item Trace routes and identify separated groups.

\end{itemize}

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}\subsection*{\ldots{}and explain why it matters.}

\begin{itemize}
\item Find a vulnerable link and test an alternate route.
\item Trace a message or a threshold rule round by round.
\item Distinguish a model's calculation from a real decision.

\end{itemize}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{A dot is a place. A line is a direct connection.}


Each dot in a network drawing is a \emph{node}. A node represents one object we choose to study. Each line is an \emph{edge}, or link, joining two nodes. In our alert model, a node is a location, and an edge means that the two locations have an established two-way communication channel.



Mathematicians also call a network a \emph{graph} and a node a \emph{vertex} (plural: vertices). This use of ``graph'' differs from a graph of temperature against time. A network drawing shows relationships; its horizontal and vertical positions need not be measured quantities.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}\subsection*{How to read this mathematical world}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Only drawn links count.} A and D may look close, but there is no direct A--D edge.
\item \emph{The drawing is not a map to scale.} A longer line does not mean a slower or longer trip. Moving a dot without changing its connections leaves this network unchanged.
\item \emph{A crossing is not automatically a junction.} You can transfer from one edge to another at a node. Lines that happen to cross without a marked node do not create another location.

\end{itemize}


We can record the same network with an \emph{edge list}: AB, AC, BC, BD, CD, DE, EF, EG, FG. ``AB'' names the link between A and B; it does not mean multiplication. Because the links work both ways, AB and BA describe the same edge, counted once.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Assumptions we will keep using},breakable,colback=warm,colbacktitle=warm,coltitle=gold,colframe=gold!35]

The main network is \emph{undirected}: every link works both ways. It is \emph{unweighted}: all links count equally. It is also \emph{simple}: there are no links from a node to itself and at most one link per pair. Those assumptions determine which formulas apply.


The seven locations and their links are an invented teaching example, not a description of an actual county emergency system.


\end{tcolorbox}


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 1},breakable]
A community center can call a shelter, but the shelter cannot call back using that channel. Would an ordinary two-way line accurately represent this relationship?
\end{tcolorbox}



\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Degree means the number of direct neighbors}


The \emph{degree} of a node counts the edges that touch it. In this simple network, that is also the number of its direct neighbors. ``Degree'' here is a count, not an angle or a temperature.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Count the school's contacts},breakable]

The edges touching D are BD, CD, and DE. Its neighbors are B, C, and E, so the degree of D is 3. We write \(\deg(D)=3\). The notation means ``the degree of node D.'' A can eventually reach D, but A is not a direct neighbor and does not count toward D's degree.


\end{tcolorbox}


\par\medskip{\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}YYYYYYYY@{}}\toprule
Node & A & B & C & D & E & F & G\\
\midrule
Degree & 2 & 3 & 3 & 3 & 3 & 2 & 2\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}}\par\medskip


\Needspace{5\baselineskip}\subsection*{Count the same links from two viewpoints}

Add the degrees: \(2+3+3+3+3+2+2=18\). Yet the edge list has only 9 links. Why twice as many? Each link has two ends. AB contributes 1 to A's count and 1 to B's count.


sum of degrees = 2 $\times$ number of edges

This is the \emph{handshake rule}. Imagine counting how many handshakes each person had: every handshake appears in two people's totals. With 9 edges, the degree sum must be \(2(9)=18\). This gives us a useful check on our counting.



We can also use the average from Week 3: the mean degree is \(18/7\approx2.57\). No individual location needs to have degree 2.57. An average summarizes the group; every actual degree here is a whole number.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 2},breakable]
A different simple undirected network has 12 edges. What must the sum of all its degrees be? If you counted 23, what would that tell you?
\end{tcolorbox}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Density compares what exists with what could exist}


Nine edges sounds like a lot until we ask how many are possible. Among seven locations, each one could have six other locations as neighbors. Counting \(7\times6=42\) lists every pair twice: once as AB and again as BA. Divide by 2 to get 21 distinct possible links.



\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.2in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week9/density-pairs.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{Keep the node count fixed when making this comparison. The network contains 9 of the 21 possible pairs. The extra line crossings in the complete drawing are not additional nodes.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip


The \emph{density} of this simple undirected network is the fraction of possible links that are present:

\[\text{density}=\frac{\text{actual edges}}{\text{possible edges}}=\frac{9}{21}=\frac{3}{7}\approx0.429.\]

As a percentage, that is about 42.9\%. It means 42.9\% of the possible \emph{pairs of locations} have direct links. It does not mean that 42.9\% of the locations are connected, or that any given message has a 42.9\% chance of arriving. We have not introduced a probability of transmission.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Read the general formula},breakable]

Let \(n\) mean the number of nodes and \(m\) the number of edges. The letters are placeholders for counts. Then


\[\text{possible edges}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2},\qquad D=\frac{m}{n(n-1)/2}=\frac{2m}{n(n-1)}.\]


For seven nodes and nine edges, substitute \(n=7\) and \(m=9\). You get \(18/(7\cdot6)=18/42=0.428571\ldots\). The letter \(D\) in the formula stands for density; it is separate from the location named D.


\end{tcolorbox}


A density of 1 means every pair has a direct link. A density of 0 means no links exist. Adding a new link to our example raises density to \(10/21\approx0.476\). Where that link goes still matters: a new link inside one region and a new link between regions increase the count equally but can have different effects on reliability.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 3},breakable]
Five nodes have four undirected edges. How many distinct pairs are possible, and what is the density?
\end{tcolorbox}



\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{A path counts the links you actually use}


A \emph{path} is a route that follows edges from one node to another without repeating nodes. In an unweighted network, its \emph{length} is the number of edges used. The \emph{distance} between two nodes is the length of a shortest path.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Get an alert from A to G},breakable]

One route is \(A\to B\to D\to E\to G\). Check each step against the edge list: AB, BD, DE, and EG all exist. The route names five nodes but uses four links, so its length is 4. The arrows here show the direction we travel along two-way links.


Another shortest route is \(A\to C\to D\to E\to G\), also length 4. There need not be just one shortest path. A route that names A--D--G is invalid: neither AD nor DG exists in the original network.


\end{tcolorbox}

\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.2in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week9/path-layers.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{Start at A with distance 0. B and C are one edge away; D is two away; E is three away; F and G are four away. These distance labels also become earliest arrival rounds under the spread rule introduced below.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}\subsection*{Connected does not mean directly connected}

The network is \emph{connected} because every pair of nodes can be joined by some path. A and G do not need a direct link for the whole network to be connected.


A \emph{connected component} is a whole group of nodes that can reach one another, with no path to nodes outside the group. Our original network has one component containing all seven nodes. If D--E is removed, there are two components: \{A, B, C, D\} and \{E, F, G\}. The braces simply mean ``the group containing these nodes.''



An isolated node, with no incident edges, is a component of one. If no path exists between two locations, their distance is \emph{unreachable}, not zero. Zero is the distance from a node to itself.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 4},breakable]
For edges PQ, QR, and ST, using nodes P, Q, R, S, T, how many components are there? What is the distance from P to R?
\end{tcolorbox}



\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{One vulnerable link can separate an entire region}


A \emph{bridge} is an edge whose removal increases the number of connected components. It need not look like a physical bridge. It is a link with a particular structural role.



D--E is a bridge: removing it changes our connected network from one component to two. By contrast, removing B--C does not disconnect the network. B can still reach C through A or D, so alternate routes remain.



\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.2in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week9/failure-repair.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{Test the failure explicitly. In the repaired drawing, A can reach G through A--B--D--F--G without using D--E. The dashed line represents the failed link, not a usable connection.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Spend the budget on an alternate route},breakable]

Suppose the county can add one new link. Adding A--D shortens a local route but leaves D--E as the only cross-region link. Adding D--F creates a second way into the right region. If D--E fails, the route A--B--D--F--G still works.


This is \emph{redundancy}: another route can serve when one link is lost. The new network is more resilient to that failure. In fact, with D--F added, no individual edge is a bridge; every edge has an alternate route around it.


\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{5\baselineskip}\subsection*{A link failure and a location failure are different tests}

Removing a node also removes every edge touching it. A node whose removal increases the number of components is an \emph{articulation point}. D and E are articulation points in the original network.


Adding D--F protects against the loss of D--E, but both cross-region routes still depend on D. If the school itself goes offline, B and C lose both connections through D. The repaired network is still vulnerable to the loss of that node. A claim about resilience should name the failure being tested.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 5},breakable]
In the three-node path P--Q--R, which edges are bridges? Which node is an articulation point?
\end{tcolorbox}



\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{``Important'' can mean several different things}


B, C, D, and E all have degree 3. Yet they do different jobs. B and C have alternate routes around them. D and E sit at the only passage between regions. Their equal degrees do not imply equal vulnerability or equal roles.



\par\medskip{\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}YYY@{}}\toprule
Idea & What you measure & Question it helps answer\\
\midrule
Degree & Number of direct neighbors & Who can contact many others in one step?\\
Closeness & Shortest-path distances to the other nodes & Who is relatively few steps from everyone?\\
Betweenness & How often shortest paths between other nodes pass through a node & Who lies along many short routes between groups?\\
Bridge or articulation role & Whether removing a link or node splits a component & Where can one failure separate the system?\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}}\par\medskip


You do not need a betweenness formula this week. Trace routes between the two regions: every such path must pass through D and E. That gives a reason for their structural importance beyond counting neighbors. A bottleneck position is not a measurement of a person's worth or the value of a neighborhood.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Extension \textperiodcentered{} Turn ``few steps away'' into an average},breakable]

From A, the distances to the six \emph{other} nodes are 1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 4. Their sum is 15. The average distance is \(15/6=2.5\) edges. From D, the distances to A, B, C, E, F, G are 2, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, whose sum is 9 and average is \(9/6=1.5\). So D is closer on average to the rest of this network.


One common \emph{closeness centrality} score reverses that average: \((n-1)/(\text{sum of distances})\). Thus A scores \(6/15=0.400\), while D scores \(6/9\approx0.667\). Larger scores mean shorter average distances. The explorer displays a dash if a node cannot reach the whole network; this particular formula assumes all those distances exist.


\end{tcolorbox}


Another optional score, \emph{degree centrality}, divides degree by the largest possible degree, \(n-1\). For D this is \(3/6=0.5\): D is directly linked to half of the six other locations. It describes a fraction of possible neighbors, not a probability of success.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 6},breakable]
If the objective is to reach the school directly from A, should you add A--D or D--F? If the objective is surviving loss of D--E, does the answer change?
\end{tcolorbox}



\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{An adjacency table answers ``Is there a direct link?''}


\emph{Adjacent} means directly connected by an edge. An \emph{adjacency matrix} is simply a square table with one row and one column for each node. A 1 records a direct connection. A 0 records its absence.


Choose the row for the starting node and the column for the other node. At row B, column D, you find 1 because BD is an edge. At row A, column D, you find 0, even though A can reach D using two links. The table records direct adjacency, not every possible path.



\par\medskip{\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}YYYYYYYY@{}}\toprule
From / to & A & B & C & D & E & F & G\\
\midrule
A & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
B & 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
C & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
D & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0\\
E & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 1\\
F & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1\\
G & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}}\par\medskip


Look at three features. The diagonal from top left to bottom right is all zeros because no node links to itself. The entries mirror one another across that diagonal because the links work both ways. The sum of a row is its node's degree: D's row has three 1s.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Why learn a second representation?},breakable,colback=warm,colbacktitle=warm,coltitle=gold,colframe=gold!35]

A drawing helps us see clusters and routes. A table lets us check entries systematically and store a network in a computer. They describe the same connections. You can read the matrix as an ordinary table; matrix algebra is not required.


\end{tcolorbox}


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 7},breakable]
Does the 0 at row A, column G mean an alert can never get from A to G?
\end{tcolorbox}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{A network shows the routes. A rule determines the spread.}


\emph{Diffusion} means something spreading through connections. To calculate it, we need more than a picture. We need an initial state and a rule for updating that state, just as we needed a starting balance and an interest rule in Week 6.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={The alert rule, stated precisely},breakable]

\begin{enumerate}
\item At round 0, only A has the alert. Round 0 is the starting state, before any sending.
\item During each round, every location that was informed \emph{at the start of that round} sends to all its direct neighbors.
\item New recipients may begin sending in the following round. All updates use the same previous snapshot.
\item Once informed, a location stays informed. Every available transmission succeeds.

\end{enumerate}

One round is one model time step. We have not said it equals a minute. That would require information about real transmission times.


\end{tcolorbox}


\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=4.1in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week9/diffusion-rounds.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{Read ``new'' as first arrivals in that round. B and C receive the alert together in round 1. They wait until round 2 to relay it onward. Nothing travels across two links within one round.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

\par\medskip{\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}YYYY@{}}\toprule
Round & Newly informed & All informed & Total\\
\midrule
0 & A (the initial source) & A & 1\\
1 & B, C & A, B, C & 3\\
2 & D & A, B, C, D & 4\\
3 & E & A, B, C, D, E & 5\\
4 & F, G & A, B, C, D, E, F, G & 7\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}}\par\medskip


Round 1 has 2 newly informed nodes and 3 informed nodes in total. Those are different counts. If B and C both send to D, D is still only one new recipient. We count locations, not message copies.



Under this exact rule, a node first receives the alert at the round equal to its shortest-path distance from A. A four-link path needs at least four rounds, and sending along a shortest path achieves that time. With D--E removed, no number of rounds can deliver A's alert to F or G because no path remains.



Real alerts can be delayed, ignored, misunderstood, or blocked by an outage. The model's timing is conditional on the rule. A deterministic spread calculation does not establish a real-world arrival guarantee.

At round 2, how many nodes are newly informed and how many are informed in total? If B and C both contact D, why do we not count two new nodes?
\end{tcolorbox}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Sometimes one informed neighbor is not enough}


An emergency relay might pass on an alert immediately. A person deciding whether to adopt a new practice may wait for support from several neighbors. A \emph{threshold model} gives a simple way to describe that different situation. ``Active'' now means a location or person has adopted the behavior; it does not merely mean a message arrived.



Use this rule: an inactive node becomes active in the next round if \emph{at least half} its neighbors are active now. Active nodes stay active. Starting active nodes are called \emph{seeds}. We again update all nodes from the same previous snapshot.



\par\medskip{\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}YYYYYYY@{}}\toprule
Number of neighbors & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6\\
\midrule
Minimum active neighbors needed & 1 & 1 & 2 & 2 & 3 & 3\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}}\par\medskip


For 3 neighbors, half is 1.5. Since we cannot have 1.5 active neighbors, at least 2 must be active. The fraction \(2/3\) passes one-half, while \(1/3\) does not. For an isolated node, this model leaves it inactive unless it is a seed; there is no neighbor influence to observe.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Why the cascade stops},breakable]

Start with B and C active. In the next round, A activates because both its neighbors are active; D activates because 2 of its 3 neighbors are active. The active set is now A, B, C, D.


E has neighbors D, F, G. Only D is active, giving \(1/3<1/2\), so E waits. F and G have no active neighbors. No one new activates in the following round. The process stops with four active nodes, although the network is connected.


\end{tcolorbox}


\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=4.1in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week9/threshold-cascade.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{Top: the half-neighbor threshold blocks spread at E. Bottom: F is also a seed, so G activates in round 1. At the start of round 2, E sees active D, F, and G, so E activates. Gold rings mark seeds or new activations.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} One extra seed changes the outcome},breakable]

Start instead with B, C, and F active. In round 1, A and D activate as before. G also activates: its neighbors are E and F, so the one active neighbor F reaches the half-neighbor threshold. E still waits in round 1 because only F was active among its neighbors at round 0.


At the start of round 2, E has three active neighbors: D, F, G. E activates, so all seven nodes are now active. The network and the rule stayed the same; adding F to the starting set changed the collective outcome.


\end{tcolorbox}


A \emph{cascade} is a sequence in which one activation helps trigger later activations. This is one meaning of a \emph{collective effect}: the result belongs to interactions across the group. A cascade can stop, spread partly, or reach everyone. It is not automatically good or bad; the meaning depends on what is spreading.



The half-neighbor rule is an invented model, not a measured law of human behavior. People may use different thresholds, and adoption can reverse. Its value here is that we can state it precisely and see what follows.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 9},breakable]
A node has five neighbors and a 60\% threshold. How many active neighbors does it need? Does that tell you whether the entire network will activate?
\end{tcolorbox}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Arrows and weights add information}


A \emph{directed} edge is drawn with an arrow. If \(A\to B\) means ``A can send to B,'' it does not also mean that B can send to A. For \(A\to B\to C\), A can reach C but C cannot return to A using those arrows. A path must obey every direction.


\emph{Out-degree} counts outgoing arrows; \emph{in-degree} counts incoming arrows. In that chain, B has one incoming and one outgoing arrow. These describe different roles. We must define what an arrow means before interpreting its direction.



A \emph{weight} is a number attached to an edge. It might represent minutes, dollars, capacity, or a multiplier. Its meaning determines how we use it.



\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.2in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week9/weighted-routes.pdf}

\end{minipage}\par\medskip


In the AI application, a weight means a multiplier applied to a numerical signal. A negative weight can subtract from the next node's sum. That is different from a negative travel time, which would not fit the road example. A weight has meaning only in its mathematical setting.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Structure, updates, and choices belong together}


The network is the \emph{structure}: who can interact with whom. The current informed or active set is the \emph{state}. The spread or threshold rule creates \emph{dynamics}: how that state changes over successive rounds. These words describe things you have already calculated.


Week 5's feedback systems combined observations with responses. A network adds the routes through which those observations and responses can travel. More sharing can produce more recipients, who then share further: an amplifying feedback process. A failed communication link can prevent a corrective message from arriving at all.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} A constrained design decision},breakable]

Weeks 7--8 asked us to choose an objective and describe allowed choices. Here the constraint is ``add one new link.'' If the objective is direct contact from A to D, A--D succeeds. If the objective is continued access across regions after D--E fails, D--F succeeds. Each adds one link and gives density 10/21, but the consequences differ.


We could test all allowed links for a chosen criterion. That would optimize the stated model; it would not decide what a community ought to value. Costs, staffing, accessibility, trust, privacy, and unequal outage risks may change the real decision.


\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{5\baselineskip}\subsection*{A practical routine for a new network}

\begin{enumerate}
\item State what each node and edge represents, including direction and weights.
\item Write an edge list or a neighbor table so the drawing can be checked.
\item Choose a question: direct contacts, travel, vulnerability, or spread.
\item Calculate with the appropriate rule and show a route, count, or sequence of states.
\item Test a proposed change against the original objective.
\item Explain one feature the model leaves out and what further information would help.

\end{enumerate}


For Week 10, keep this habit: a precise local rule can generate a result that is hard to guess from a quick glance. Networks gave us cascades across locations. Next week, repeated number and shape rules will give us other surprising patterns.

\section{AI connection: signals along weighted arrows}
Imagine a small program combining two indoor sensor scores. The heat score is $x_1=2$ and the motion score is $x_2=4$. The subscripts identify two different inputs. These invented scores are rescaled to numbers from 0 to 6; they are not temperatures or probabilities.

The diagram is a tiny \emph{linear signal network}. Every arrow has a multiplier called a weight. Each receiving node adds its incoming weighted signals. The intermediate nodes $H_1,H_2$ form a hidden layer: a set of calculations between the inputs and output. ``Hidden'' means intermediate, not secret.
\begin{center}\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.2in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week9/neural-signals.pdf}\end{center}
\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Worked calculation: multiply, then add}]
At the first hidden node,
\[
H_1=0.50x_1+0.25x_2=0.50(2)+0.25(4)=1+1=2.
\]
At the other hidden node, one contribution is negative:
\[
H_2=-0.50x_1+0.75x_2=-0.50(2)+0.75(4)=-1+3=2.
\]
Now send those values through the two arrows to the output:
\[
O=1.50H_1-0.50H_2=1.50(2)-0.50(2)=3-1=2.
\]
The sign of a weight changes a contribution; the arrow determines the direction. A weight is a multiplier, not a transmission probability.
\end{tcolorbox}

\clearpage
\subsection*{A connected input can have no net effect}
Hold $x_1$ fixed and increase $x_2$ by 1. The path through $H_1$ adds $0.25(1.50)=0.375$ to the output. The path through $H_2$ adds $0.75(-0.50)=-0.375$. The effects cancel exactly. Changing the motion score changes both hidden values but leaves the output unchanged.

In fact, substituting the hidden formulas into the output gives
\[
O=1.50(0.50x_1+0.25x_2)-0.50(-0.50x_1+0.75x_2)=x_1.
\]
This algebra is an extension; the two path contributions already explain the cancellation. Connections show where influence \emph{can} travel. The weights determine how those influences combine.

\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Remove one edge and test again}]
With the original inputs 2 and 4, remove the arrow $x_2\to H_1$. The new hidden values are $H_1=0.50(2)=1$ and $H_2=2$. Thus $O=1.50(1)-0.50(2)=0.5$. The other motion-score path remains, so the earlier cancellation is broken.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection*{An activation rule adds another transformation}
The original model only multiplies by constants and adds. Combining such linear calculations still gives a linear relationship. Neural networks commonly add \emph{activation functions}, rules that transform a node's sum before sending it onward.

For example, ReLU keeps a nonnegative sum and replaces a negative sum by zero. With $x_1=4,x_2=0$, the raw hidden sums are 2 and $-2$. Without the extra rule, the output is $1.50(2)-0.50(-2)=4$. With ReLU at the hidden nodes only, the sent values are 2 and 0, so the output is 3. This introduces a nonlinear relationship. Real models also commonly add adjustable constants called biases; our model sets them to zero.

\subsection*{A forward pass is different from training}
A \emph{forward pass} uses current inputs and weights to calculate an output. Training uses examples with targets, compares outputs with those targets, and changes parameters in response to errors. That makes training a feedback process.

For the original prediction 2 and an invented target 3, define signed error as target minus prediction: $3-2=1$. If we change only the $H_1$-to-output weight from 1.50 to 2.00, the new output is $2.00(2)-0.50(2)=3$. This hand-chosen change matches one target; it does not establish accuracy on other examples. Backpropagation and an optimization method organize parameter updates in larger models.

The weights, scores, and targets here are invented and have not been fitted to sensor data. The output is a toy score. The online AI application lets you change inputs, remove an edge, and compare the two node rules.
