A \$2,000 balance can grow through fixed dollar additions or a percentage of its current amount. A loan combines percentage growth with payments that subtract from the debt. Tables and graphs make the differences visible.

This chapter develops the meaning of percentages, period rates, and repeated updates before using formulas. It distinguishes an ending balance from interest earned, a scheduled payment from principal reduction, and a simulation's stopping month from an actual payoff.

A fixed-dollar addition gives linear growth, while a percentage of the current balance gives compound growth. A loan combines percentage growth with payments, and the order of the updates affects the next balance.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{A rate needs an amount to act on and a period to describe}

\emph{Principal} means the initial amount deposited or borrowed. We use P for that starting amount and B for a balance as it changes. A \emph{period} is one update interval, such as a year or month. Year 0 records the initial balance, before any annual interest is added.


A percentage means ``per hundred.'' To calculate 3\% of \$2,000, first write \(3\%=3/100=0.03\), then multiply: \(0.03(2000)=60\) dollars. To keep the original money and add that interest, multiply by \(1+0.03=1.03\).


\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=2.9in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week6/percent-and-factor.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{The rate 0.03 gives the interest portion. The factor 1.03 keeps the original balance and adds 3\% to it.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

\par\medskip{\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}YYY@{}}\toprule
Expression & Meaning & For a \$2,000 balance\\
\midrule
0.03B & 3\% of the balance & Interest of \$60\\
B + 0.03B & Keep the balance and add its interest & New balance \$2,060\\
1.03B & The same update written as multiplication & New balance \$2,060\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}}\par\medskip

``3\% per year'' and ``3\% per month'' describe different updates. In a formula, use a decimal rate whose time unit matches the period. Also keep separate the \emph{interest amount} and the \emph{total balance}: earning \$60 does not mean the whole account is worth \$60.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Read the notation before calculating},breakable]

\(B_0=2000\) names the starting balance. \(B_1\) means the balance after one update, and \(B_2\) after two. The small number is a period label, not a multiplier. The rule \(B_{k+1}=1.03B_k\) says ``multiply the current balance by 1.03 to get the next one.''


\end{tcolorbox}

\end{tcolorbox}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Simple interest adds the same dollar amount each year}

With \emph{simple interest}, interest is calculated on the original principal, not on the accumulated balance. Start at P=\$2,000 with annual rate r=0.03. Each year adds the same \(Pr=60\) dollars.


\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=2.9in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week6/simple-updates.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{The equal dollar steps make a straight-line pattern. The year-5 balance includes the original \$2,000 plus five \$60 additions.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

After t years, interest is \(I=Prt\), and total amount is

\[A=P+Prt=P(1+rt).\]
The formula collects the repeated additions into one calculation. With annual updates, we can also write \(B_{k+1}=B_k+Pr\): the constant step Pr is added to the current total, but calculated from the \emph{original} principal.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Evaluate and solve backward},breakable]

After five years, \(A=2000(1+0.03\cdot5)=2300\) dollars. The interest earned is \(2300-2000=300\) dollars.


Suppose instead a simple-interest account grows from \$500 to \$560 in four years. The \$60 gain is \$15 per year. Dividing \$15 by the \$500 original amount gives \(r=0.03\), or 3\% per year. Algebra gives the same answer: \(560/500-1=4r\), so \(r=0.12/4=0.03\).


\end{tcolorbox}

If time is given in months while r is annual, convert months to years. Under a proportional simple-interest model, six months is t=0.5 year; \$2,000 at 3\% earns \(2000(0.03)(0.5)=30\) dollars over that time.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 2},breakable]
An account starts at \$900 and earns 4\% simple annual interest. What is the fixed yearly increase and the total after three years?
\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Compound interest lets earlier interest earn interest}

With \emph{compound interest}, each update calculates interest on the balance reached so far. At 3\% annually, \$2,000 becomes \$2,060 after one year. The second year's interest is \(0.03(2060)=61.80\), so the next balance is \$2,121.80.


The annual rule is \(B_{k+1}=(1+r)B_k\). Applying it repeatedly gives

\[B_1=P(1+r),\quad B_2=P(1+r)^2,\quad A=P(1+r)^t.\]
The exponent counts repeated multiplications. For three years, multiply by 1.03 three times; do not replace this by adding 3\% of the original amount three times.

\end{minipage}\par\medskip

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Monthly compounding changes both the rate and the count}

Suppose a model quotes a nominal annual rate r=0.06 and compounds 12 times a year. The rate per monthly update is \(i=r/12=0.005\), which is 0.5\%, not 6\%. Over ten years there are \(12(10)=120\) monthly updates.


For n equal compounding periods per year and t years, the formula is

\[A=P\left(1+\frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}.\]
Read the inside and outside together: r/n is the rate for one step, and nt is the number of steps. Using a monthly rate with an annual count would model a different amount of time.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Write the substituted expression first},breakable]

For \$1,000 at 6\% nominal annual interest compounded monthly for ten years:


\[A=1000\left(1+\frac{0.06}{12}\right)^{120}=1000(1.005)^{120}\approx1819.40.\]


Use parentheses around the full factor and keep the calculator's precision through the exponent. The final interest earned is approximately \$819.40.


\end{tcolorbox}

\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=2.9in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week6/compounding-frequency.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{The bars show interest earned, not the whole account balance. The comparison holds the nominal annual rate fixed. Daily means 365 equal periods in this constructed model.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

For these positive rates, more frequent compounding increases the final amount, with smaller extra gains as the frequencies increase. This comparison does not hold the effective annual yield fixed.


\Needspace{5\baselineskip}\subsection*{The effective annual yield describes a whole year's growth}

The \emph{effective annual yield} is the fraction gained over one year, including compounding. For a 3\% nominal annual rate compounded monthly,


\[\left(1+\frac{0.03}{12}\right)^{12}-1\approx0.030416,\]


or about 3.0416\%. The decimal 0.030416 and the percentage 3.0416\% describe the same yield. The nominal rate 3\% and the effective yield answer different questions about the update rule.

\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{The Rule of 72 estimates repeated percentage growth}

For a positive balance growing at a fixed positive annual compound rate, the \emph{Rule of 72} estimates the years needed to double:

\[\text{doubling time}\approx\frac{72}{\text{annual rate written as a percentage number}}.\]
At 6\%, use 72/6=12 years. Do not divide by 0.06. At 9\%, the estimate is eight years. These are estimates under a continuing growth rule, not guaranteed future returns.


\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=2.9in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week6/doubling-estimates.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{About 12 years produces one doubling, and about 24 produces two doublings. Two doublings multiply the starting amount by four, not by three.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Check the estimate using powers},breakable]

At 6\% annual compounding, \((1.06)^{11}\approx1.898\) and \((1.06)^{12}\approx2.012\). The first completed annual update reaching twice the starting balance is year 12. The Rule of 72 is a useful scale estimate; it is not an equation making the balance exactly double at year 12.


\end{tcolorbox}

The same reasoning works for invented compute capacity or another quantity following a fixed percentage rule. It does not describe simple interest, and it does not by itself describe a loan being reduced by payments. At a zero growth rate, a positive starting amount never doubles through this rule.

\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{A payment subtracts from a balance that has just grown}

In our loan model, interest is added once a month, then the scheduled payment is applied. Let B be the balance at the start of that month, i the monthly decimal rate, and M the regular payment in dollars.

\[\text{interest}=iB,\qquad B_{\text{next}}=B+iB-M=(1+i)B-M.\]
The annual rate r is divided by 12 \emph{under the stated monthly model}, so i=r/12. A \$4,000 loan at a 6\% nominal annual rate uses i=0.06/12=0.005, or 0.5\% per month. The first interest charge is \$20. In credit-card terminology, APR stands for \emph{annual percentage rate}; our card exercises explicitly specify the monthly conversion used in their simplified model.


\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=2.9in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week6/loan-one-month.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{The \$200 payment covers \$20 interest and reduces principal by \$180. The ending balance \$3,820 becomes the next month's starting balance.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Repeat using the new balance},breakable]

Month 1: \(4000(1.005)-200=3820\). Month 2 starts at \$3,820, so its interest is \(3820(0.005)=19.10\) dollars. After the next \$200 payment, the balance is \(3820+19.10-200=3639.10\).


Using \$4,000 again in month 2 would incorrectly keep charging the original interest amount. The current balance is part of the rule.


\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[title={The credit-card examples use a simplified schedule},breakable,colback=warm,colbacktitle=warm,coltitle=gold,colframe=gold!35]

Our activities use fixed rates, no new purchases or fees, and one interest calculation followed by one payment each month. The \href{https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-does-my-credit-card-company-calculate-the-amount-of-interest-i-owe-en-51/}{Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains} that many real card issuers use daily balance calculations. The monthly model makes the repeated arithmetic visible; actual statements follow their account terms.


\end{tcolorbox}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Interest and principal reduction add up to the actual payment}

For the decreasing loan above, the monthly payment stays \$200, but its parts change. A smaller starting balance generates less interest. More of the same payment can then reduce what is owed.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{The final payment is the amount still due}

Continue the \$4,000 loan at 6\% with \$200 monthly payments, rounding monthly interest to cents. After 21 regular payments, \$24.87 remains. Month 22 adds \$0.12 interest, so the final amount due is \$24.99. Paying that amount closes the loan.


We do not subtract the full scheduled \$200 and report a negative debt. The actual last payment is the smaller of the regular payment and the amount due.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Total paid and total interest},breakable]

\[\text{total paid}=21(200)+24.99=4224.99,\]


\[\text{total interest}=4224.99-4000=224.99.\]


The total paid includes the \$4,000 originally borrowed. The amount above that principal is the interest in this model, since there are no other fees or charges.


\end{tcolorbox}

\end{minipage}\par\medskip

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Growing, unchanged, and decreasing are different outcomes}

A payment does not automatically make a debt smaller. From \(B_{\text{next}}-B=iB-M\), compare M with the interest iB. If the payment is larger, the balance falls. If it is equal, the balance stays the same. If it is smaller, the balance grows.


\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=2.9in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week6/payment-threshold.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{The first interest charge is \$10 for all three plans. A \$10 payment covers that interest exactly and leaves the original \$1,000 unchanged; it does not pay the loan off.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

With a fixed rate, fixed payment, and no new charges, a payment exceeding the initial monthly interest makes the balance decrease. Later interest is then no larger, so the same payment continues reducing the balance. In a fixed-rate model, this leads to eventual payoff; a calculator still needs enough periods to display the full path.


If a simulation stops at a chosen horizon, such as 1,200 months, an outstanding balance means ``not paid within this horizon.'' It must not label the stopping month as payoff. At zero interest, a positive payment reduces the loan by a fixed amount each month; a zero payment leaves it unchanged.

\end{tcolorbox}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{A useful comparison includes the path, the totals, and the assumptions}

A monthly payment, payoff time, and total interest answer different questions. For a borrower, the payment describes the current monthly commitment and the total describes its long-run modeled cost. A lender can use the same schedule to calculate payments received and interest collected. The underlying arithmetic is shared.


When comparing plans, name the starting principal, rate per period, timing of payments, rounding rule, and whether new purchases or fees are included. Then report what the model actually computes. Changing one assumption gives a new scenario, not a correction to a fact about every loan.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} A complete comparison statement},breakable]

``For a \$4,000 balance at 6\% nominal annual interest with monthly updates, no additional charges, and cent-rounded monthly interest, \$300 payments give payoff in month 14 and \$150.01 total interest. With \$150 payments, payoff is month 29 and interest is \$303.84.'' This states both the result and the conditions supporting it.


\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Define the balance and the time unit.
\item Match the decimal rate to that unit.
\item Calculate one update by hand before using a formula or calculator.
\item Trace enough periods to answer the question.
\item Separate the ending balance, interest, payments, and total cost.
\item State a comparison and an assumption worth checking.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{A fixed repayment comparison with full precision}
The online application starts with an \$8,000 balance, a nominal annual rate of 18\%, and monthly interest $i=0.015$. Interest is added first; fixed payments follow. There are no purchases, fees, or rate changes.

Both plans incur \$120 first-month interest. A \$160 payment reduces principal by \$40 and ends at \$7,960. A \$300 payment reduces principal by \$180 and ends at \$7,820. In month 2, their interest charges are \$119.40 and \$117.30 because the starting balances now differ.

\subsection*{A resource index can follow the same growth mathematics}
An invented computing-capacity index begins at 100 units. One plan adds 25 units each year: $A_k=100+25k$. A second grows by 25\%: $B_k=100(1.25)^k$.

Both give 125 after one year. After two, the additive plan gives 150 but percentage growth gives 156.25. Its second increase is 31.25 because it acts on the updated base 125. These are resource-index values, not counts of whole machines or measurements of intelligence.

Suppose a third plan grows by 25\% until a ceiling 400 is reached. Its rule is
\[
L_0=100,\qquad L_{k+1}=\min(400,1.25L_k).
\]
Here min means choose the smaller value. At year 6, the limited and uncapped percentage plans both have about 381.47. At year 7, the uncapped value is about 476.84 while the limited rule selects 400.

\begin{center}\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../assets/week6/ai-growth-limits.pdf}\end{center}
The ceiling changes the rule and thus the trajectory. It is an explicit hypothetical policy, not a forecast about any real AI operation. Learning, reliability, energy, and useful service each require suitable measures and observations.

\section*{Chapter 6 Review}
Show units and at least one substituted expression or update. For savings formulas, keep precision until the final displayed answer. For loan tables, use monthly cent rounding unless the question explicitly says full precision. Assume no fees or new borrowing unless stated.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \$5,000 account earns 4\% simple interest annually. Write its balance formula, give balances for years 0 through 5, and find the time to \$6,000. What is the fixed yearly interest? Also recover the simple annual rate when \$500 becomes \$560 in four years.
\item The same \$5,000 instead earns 4\% compounded annually. Give balances for years 0 through 5, compare the interest in year 1 with year 5, and find how much more it holds after five years than the simple model. Explain the source of the extra gain.

\subsection*{Chapter review: solutions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item $A(t)=5000(1+0.04t)=5000+200t$. Balances are 5000, 5200, 5400, 5600, 5800, 6000 dollars, so \$6,000 is reached at year 5. Each year adds \$200 based on the original principal. For \$500 growing to \$560 in four simple-interest years, $r=(560/500-1)/4=0.03$, or 3\% annually.
\item $B_t=5000(1.04)^t$ gives 5000.00, 5200.00, 5408.00, 5624.32, 5849.29, 6083.26 dollars. Year 1 earns \$200; year 5 earns approximately \$233.97. The extra balance over the simple model is approximately \$83.26. Earlier interest is included in later percentage calculations; the rate itself stays at 4\%.
\item Final balances are approximately 1790.85 annually, 1814.02 quarterly, 1819.40 monthly, and 1822.03 daily. Subtract 1000 to get interest 790.85, 814.02, 819.40, and 822.03 dollars. The monthly decimal rate is 0.06/12=0.005 and the count is 120. The comparison fixes the nominal annual rate, starting amount, and duration.
\item Quarterly rate is 0.06/4=0.015, or 1.5\%, with 8 factors. $1000(1.015)^8\approx1126.49$. The effective annual yield at 3\% nominal with monthly compounding is $(1+0.03/12)^{12}-1\approx0.030416$, or about 3.0416\%. Multiply the decimal yield by 100 to express it as a percentage.
\item (a) $72/12=6$ years. (b) $72/7\approx10.29$ years. (c) About $2(72/7)\approx20.57$ years. (d) About 12\% annually. The Rule of 72 is an approximation for continuing percentage growth, not an equality identifying an exact balance on a particular crediting date.
\item One estimated doubling gives approximately \$60,000. Direct calculation gives $30000(1.06)^{12}\approx60365.89$. Factors at years 11 and 12 are about 1.898 and 2.012. Thus the first completed annual update reaching twice the start is year 12. The small excess over two is consistent with the rule being an estimate.
\item Interest/end-balance pairs for months 1--4 are (25.00,2405.00), (24.05,2309.05), (23.09,2212.14), and (22.12,2114.26), all dollars. Subtract each interest from 120 to get principal reductions 95.00, 95.95, 96.91, and 97.88. A constant \$95 reduction gives $2500/95\approx26.32$ months, or 27 whole payments to clear the amount under that simplified rule. The actual schedule finishes in 24 months because the declining balance reduces interest and increases the principal part of later payments.
\item Month 1 is interest 20.00, payment 200, end 3820.00. Month 2 is interest 19.10, payment 200, end 3639.10. After 21 payments, 24.87 remains. Month 22 adds 0.12 interest, so actual payment is 24.99 and end is zero. Total paid is $21(200)+24.99=4224.99$; total interest is 224.99. Twenty-two full payments would count \$175.01 that is not owed under this schedule.

\item Both first interest charges are \$25. With a \$150 payment the first end is \$4,875; with \$250 it is \$4,775. Second interest is \$24.38 versus \$23.88 after cent rounding. Its dollar increases grow with its base, while A continues adding the fixed \$50.
