Number Systems: Counting, Movement, Scaling, and Recursion

Numbers are more than marks on a page. They describe quantities, changes, proportions, and patterns that unfold one step at a time.

How to use this reading

Read the explanations in order. Each new idea is introduced through a situation, followed by the mathematics that describes it. When you reach a practice question, try answering in your own words or on paper before opening the solution. The original textbook PDF remains available for further examples and exercises.

1. Why we have several kinds of numbers

Suppose you count seven books. The number \(7\) is a natural number: it tells how many objects are present. Suppose no books remain. You need \(0\). Suppose you owe someone three dollars. You need \(-3\). Suppose you share one pizza equally among four people. You need \(\frac14\). Each situation asks numbers to do a slightly different job.

The familiar number tower organizes these enlargements. Natural numbers are used for counting; whole numbers include zero; integers also include negative numbers; rational numbers include fractions and terminating or repeating decimals; real numbers also include numbers such as \(\sqrt2\) and \(\pi\).

Worked example. Classify \(-4,\ 0,\ \frac38,\ \sqrt{5}\). The number \(-4\) is an integer, and therefore rational and real. The number \(0\) is whole, integer, rational, and real. The fraction \(\frac38\) is rational and real. The number \(\sqrt5\) is irrational and real. A number may belong to several nested systems.
Check your understanding. Explain why \(3\) is also rational, even though it is written without a fraction.
Show explanationIt can be written as \(3/1\), a ratio of integers with a nonzero denominator.

2. The additive view: numbers as steps

Imagine standing at \(0\) on a number line. Adding \(5\) means moving five units to the right. Adding \(-3\) means moving three units to the left. This is the additive view of numbers: a signed number describes a change in position.

Worked example. A hiker is at elevation \(-20\) feet relative to sea level. She climbs \(35\) feet. Her new elevation is \(-20+35=15\) feet. The result is positive because the upward change is larger than the initial distance below sea level.

Subtracting a number is equivalent to adding its opposite. For example, \(6-(-2)=6+2=8\). On the number line, subtracting a negative number moves us to the right.

Try it. A submarine is at \(-45\) meters and rises \(18\) meters. Where is it now?
Check\(-45+18=-27\) meters.

3. The multiplicative view: numbers as scaling

Addition describes a fixed amount of change. Multiplication describes a scale factor. To multiply a length by \(2\) is to double it; to multiply by \(0.5\) is to halve it. These are different kinds of changes.

Percentages are scale factors in disguise. An increase of \(20\%\) means that the new quantity is \(120\%\) of the old quantity, or \(1.20\) times as large. A decrease of \(20\%\) means multiplying by \(0.80\).

Worked example. A jacket costs $50. Its price rises by \(10\%\). The increase is \(0.10(50)=5\), so the new price is $55. Equivalently, \(1.10(50)=55\). If the new price then falls by \(10\%\), it becomes \(0.90(55)=49.50\), not $50. The second percentage is taken from a different starting amount.
A useful distinction. Adding $5 every week produces equal dollar changes. Increasing a balance by \(5\%\) every week produces changes that get larger as the balance grows. This distinction returns later in the course.

4. Fractions, equivalence, and measurement

Different-looking fractions may describe the same number. For instance, \(\frac12=\frac24=\frac{50}{100}\). Multiplying the numerator and denominator by the same nonzero number changes the notation without changing the value.

To add fractions with different denominators, first rewrite them in a common unit: \(\frac13+\frac14=\frac4{12}+\frac3{12}=\frac7{12}\). To multiply fractions, multiply the numerators and the denominators.

Worked example. A recipe uses \(\frac34\) cup of oats, and you want to make two-thirds of the recipe. Multiply: \(\frac23\cdot\frac34=\frac6{12}=\frac12\) cup. This is multiplication as scaling, not addition.
Try it. Explain why \(\frac6{10}\) and \(0.6\) name the same number.
Check\(6/10\) means six tenths, which is written \(0.6\) in decimal notation.

5. Operations on real numbers

Order matters when several operations appear in one expression. Grouping symbols such as parentheses come first, then exponents, multiplication and division, and addition and subtraction. Operations at the same level are evaluated left to right.

The distributive property, \(a(b+c)=ab+ac\), describes how multiplication interacts with addition. It lets us simplify expressions, check calculations, and translate everyday situations into equations.

Worked example. A movie club charges a $12 annual fee plus $4 per visit. For \(n\) visits the cost is \(12+4n\). If three friends each join and make \(n\) visits, the total is \(3(12+4n)=36+12n\). The distributive property separates membership costs from visit costs.

A mathematical expression has a meaning before any numbers are substituted. Learning to interpret \(12+4n\) as “starting cost plus repeated cost” is as important as evaluating it.

6. Recursion: a rule that uses its own past

A recursion tells us how to obtain a new value from an earlier one. We must know the starting value and the update rule. Unlike a one-time calculation, a recursive rule can be repeated to produce a sequence.

For an additive recursion, \(a_{n+1}=a_n+3\) with \(a_0=2\), the sequence is \(2,5,8,11,\ldots\). Each step adds exactly three.

For a multiplicative recursion, \(b_{n+1}=2b_n\) with \(b_0=2\), the sequence is \(2,4,8,16,\ldots\). Each step doubles the previous value.

Worked comparison. Both sequences begin at \(2\). After three updates, the additive model gives \(11\), while the multiplicative model gives \(16\). Repeating a percentage change can eventually produce much faster growth than repeating a fixed addition.
Looking ahead. In Week 5, feedback rules will use a current measurement to decide the next value. Recursion gives us the arithmetic language for describing that process.
Try it. Starting at \(5\), compute four updates of \(a_{n+1}=a_n-2\).
CheckStart at \(5\), then \(3,1,-1,-3\).
