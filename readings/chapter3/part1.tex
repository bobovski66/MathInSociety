

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{What are the numbers about?}

A list of measurements is a \emph{data set}. Each number describes an observation: perhaps one day's waiting time or the amount in one bottle. Before calculating, ask what was measured, which units were used, and who or what the list describes.


A measure of \emph{center} describes the typical location of the observations. A measure of \emph{spread} describes how far apart they are. For a useful comparison, report both and consider the shape of the data.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={The same average can describe different experiences},breakable]

One route to work takes 24, 25, and 26 minutes. Another takes 15, 25, and 35 minutes. Both average 25 minutes. The second route varies more, with trips taking as long as 35 minutes. If arriving on time matters, you will want to know about that variation.


\end{tcolorbox}

Units matter. Before averaging travel times, for example, convert all measurements to minutes or all to hours. Comparisons between different groups also require knowing how the observations were selected.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 1},breakable]
What is missing from ``The average was 25''?
\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{The mean: share the total equally}

Add the values and divide by how many there are. This gives the \emph{mean}, often called the average. Imagine sharing the total equally among all the observations.

\[\bar x=\frac{\text{sum of the values}}{\text{number of values}}=\frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n}{n}.\]
We write the sample mean as \(\bar x\), pronounced 'x-bar'. The symbol \(n\) is the number of observations, and \(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\) label their values. The mean equals their sum divided by \(n\).


\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=2.65in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week3/mean-sharing.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{The mean preserves the total: number of observations $\times$ mean = original total.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

\begin{tcolorbox}[title={A mean does not have to appear in the list},breakable]

For 2, 3, 3, 5, and 7 hours, the total is 20 and the mean is 20 $\div$ 5 = 4 hours. The mean of 4 hours describes the group as a whole, even though none of the observations equals 4.


You can reverse the calculation. If four values have mean 12, their total must be 4 $\times$ 12 = 48. If three are 9, 11, and 13, the missing value is 48 $-$ 33 = 15.


\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 2},breakable]
The values are 4, 6, and 8 minutes. Find the mean, then recover their total from the mean.
\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{The median: find the middle after ordering}

Put the observations in order before finding the \emph{median}. With an odd number of values, choose the middle position. With an even number, average the two middle values.


\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=2.7in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week3/median-positions.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{Count positions in the ordered list. The middle of the list is not necessarily halfway between the smallest and largest measurements.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Sort first},breakable]

The values 9, 2, 4, 4, 7, and 5 become 2, 4, 4, 5, 7, 9. There are six observations. Positions 3 and 4 contain 4 and 5, so the median is (4 + 5)/2 = 4.5.


\end{tcolorbox}

Repeated values matter. In 1, 2, 2, 2, 9, the median is 2. There are not equal numbers \emph{strictly} below and above it because several observations equal it. The ordered-position procedure still works.


The median depends on position in the sorted list. Changing a value far above the middle may change the mean substantially while leaving the median unchanged. This can make the median useful for data with unusually large or small values.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 3},breakable]
Find the median of 3, 8, 1, and 6.
\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{The mode: count repeated values}

The \emph{mode} is a most frequently occurring value. A frequency table makes the count visible. There can be several tied modes.


\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=2.65in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week3/mode-counts.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{For this course, report ``no mode'' when every observed value appears once. A tie among repeated values can give more than one mode.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

Mean, median, and mode answer different questions. The mean shares the total; the median finds the middle; the mode finds a most common value. They can agree, but they do not have to.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Use the question to choose the summary},breakable]

A shop planning its most commonly requested size might care about the mode. To divide a total cost equally, the mean answers the question. To describe a middle amount when a few amounts are unusually large, the median may be more helpful.


\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 4},breakable]
What are the modes of 1, 1, 2, 3, and 3?
\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{The range: measure the full width}

The \emph{range} is the maximum minus the minimum. It has the same units as the observations.

\[\text{range}=\text{largest value}-\text{smallest value}.\]
\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Watch the subtraction sign},breakable]

For $-$3, 1, and 4 degrees, the range is 4 $-$ ($-$3) = 7 degrees. For 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, the range is 12 $-$ 8 = 4. For 2, 6, 10, 14, 18, it is 18 $-$ 2 = 16. Those last two lists both have mean 10.


\end{tcolorbox}

The range measures the distance between the smallest and largest observations. The graph shows how the other values are distributed. Two lists can therefore have the same mean and the same range while distributing their observations differently.

\captionof*{figure}{Both ranges are 10. Looking at the observations reveals information the two endpoints miss.}

\end{tcolorbox}
