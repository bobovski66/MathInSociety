1. Populations, samples, and the question being asked

Suppose a college wants to know how students travel to campus. The population is the entire group the college wants to understand. A sample is the smaller group from which it actually collects information.

We study samples because measuring everyone is often too difficult or expensive. However, the way a sample is collected matters. Asking only students who arrive at a bicycle rack would give a misleading picture of how the whole college travels.

A simple random sample is selected so that every possible group of a specified size has an equal chance of being chosen. That is one way to reduce systematic selection bias.

Worked example. A college has 1,600 students. A survey includes 80 students randomly selected from its enrollment list. The population size is 1,600; the sample size is 80. Responses may help estimate the college-wide pattern, but they do not guarantee an exact population answer.
Try it. Why might asking only students in a morning class produce a biased estimate of the percentage of students who work evenings?
CheckThe students who are available for morning classes may differ systematically from students who take only afternoon or evening classes.

2. Frequency tables turn observations into counts

The frequency of an outcome is how many times it occurs. A frequency table places each outcome beside its count. Tables make a long list of responses easier to read and help us avoid counting anything twice.

Worked example. Twenty-five students choose a preferred study location. Eleven choose the library, nine choose home, and five choose a café.
LocationFrequency
Library11
Home9
Café5
Total25

The total \(11+9+5=25\) must agree with the number of observations. A missing or duplicated response would change that total.

Notice that a frequency is a count, not yet a percentage or probability.

3. Relative frequency connects data to probability

The relative frequency of an outcome is its count divided by the total number of observations. It answers the question “What fraction of the observations had this outcome?”

\[\text{Relative frequency}=\frac{\text{frequency of outcome}}{\text{total frequency}}.\]

Worked example. In the study-location survey, the library has relative frequency \(\frac{11}{25}=0.44=44\%\). Home has \(9/25=0.36\) and café has \(5/25=0.20\). The relative frequencies add to \(1\), or \(100\%\).

In a repeatable random process, relative frequencies from many trials can help estimate probabilities. But a survey proportion describes observed responses; it is not automatically a permanent law about all students.

Try it. If 18 of 60 observations fall into one category, what is its relative frequency?
Check\(18/60=0.30=30\%\).

4. Choosing a graph: bar charts and histograms

A bar chart displays counts or percentages for categories such as library, home, and café. Separate bars emphasize that the categories are distinct.

A histogram is different. It groups numerical measurements into intervals, often called bins. The bars usually touch because neighboring intervals belong to the same numerical scale.

Worked example. Suppose we record commute times in minutes. Instead of drawing a bar for every exact time, we count commuters in intervals: 0–9 minutes, 10–19 minutes, 20–29 minutes, and so on. Those interval counts form a histogram.

Changing the width of the intervals can change how the distribution looks. A very wide bin may hide detail; very narrow bins may make ordinary random fluctuations seem important.

Try it. Would you use a bar chart or a histogram to show the frequencies of eye colors? How about commute times?
CheckEye colors: bar chart for categories. Commute times: histogram for numerical intervals.

5. Probability distributions describe possible outcomes

A probability distribution lists possible outcomes along with their probabilities. Each probability must lie between \(0\) and \(1\), and the probabilities of all mutually exclusive possibilities must sum to \(1\).

Worked example. Suppose a small spinner has three labeled sections with probabilities \(P(A)=0.2\), \(P(B)=0.3\), and \(P(C)=0.5\). The total is \(0.2+0.3+0.5=1\). In a long run of spins, we might expect roughly half of the outcomes to be \(C\), but that does not require exactly half in every short run.

The complement of an event means that the event does not happen. Because an event and its complement cover all possibilities, \(P(\text{not }A)=1-P(A)\).

Try it. If the chance of a particular outcome is \(0.15\), what is the chance of not obtaining it?
Check\(1-0.15=0.85\), or \(85\%\).

6. Reading a distribution as a description, not a guarantee

Even when a probability is known, we cannot say with certainty what a single observation will be. Probability describes a pattern across possible outcomes or repeated trials.

A useful habit is to separate three statements: what was observed (frequency), what fraction was observed (relative frequency), and what is expected under a model (probability). The distinction prevents us from treating a small sample as perfect knowledge of the population.
