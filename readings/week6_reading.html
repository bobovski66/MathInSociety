Interest and Loans: How Balances Change Over Time

A loan and a savings account may start with the same number. What happens next depends on the rule that updates the balance.

1. Start with a balance and an update rule

In Week 5, we examined feedback: a system observes its current condition and uses that information in deciding what happens next. Financial balances are another example of a quantity that changes through repeated updates.

Interest is money added to a balance according to a percentage rate. The amount added depends on which balance is used and how often interest is calculated.

Suppose you deposit $1,000 at an annual rate of 5%. Five percent of $1,000 is $50. If that same $50 is added every year, the balance follows a fixed-step pattern. If interest is calculated on the growing balance, the amount added changes with time.

Recall Week 1: An additive rule adds a fixed amount. A multiplicative rule scales the current amount. Both are recursive patterns, and each describes a different financial situation.

2. Simple interest: a fixed amount added

With simple interest, interest is calculated only on the original principal. If \(P\) is the starting amount, \(r\) is the annual interest rate written as a decimal, and \(t\) is the number of years, then

\[A=P(1+rt).\]

This formula combines the original amount \(P\) with the added interest \(Prt\). At a fixed rate, the dollar increase per year is constant.

Worked example. Deposit $1,500 at 4% simple interest for three years. Convert 4% to \(0.04\), then calculate \(A=1500(1+0.04\cdot3)=1500(1.12)=\$1680\). The interest earned is $180.

Every year adds \(0.04(1500)=\$60\), so the balances at years 0, 1, 2, and 3 are $1,500, $1,560, $1,620, and $1,680.

Try it. Find the balance after five years when $800 earns 6% simple interest annually.
Check\(800(1+0.06\cdot5)=\$1040\).

3. Compound interest: the balance feeds the next increase

With compound interest, new interest is calculated on the current balance, including interest that was added earlier. This is a multiplicative update. At 5% annual compounding, the rule is

\[B_{\text{next}}=1.05B_{\text{current}}.\]

Worked example. Start with $1,000 at 5% annual compounding. After one year the balance is \(1000(1.05)=\$1050\). After two years it is \(1050(1.05)=\$1102.50\). The second year's interest is $52.50 rather than $50, because it is based on a larger balance.

The general formula for compounding \(n\) times per year over \(t\) years is

\[A=P\left(1+\frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}.\]

For annual compounding, \(n=1\), so this becomes \(A=P(1+r)^t\). More frequent compounding usually increases the final amount slightly when the quoted nominal annual rate is the same.

Try it. At 10% annual compounding, what is the balance after two years on a $200 deposit?
Check\(200(1.10)^2=\$242\).

4. The Rule of 72: estimating doubling time

Exponential growth can be difficult to estimate in your head. The Rule of 72 offers a quick approximation for the number of years needed for a balance to double:

\[\text{Doubling time}\approx \frac{72}{\text{annual percentage rate}}.\]

Use the rate as a percentage number, not a decimal. At 6%, \(72/6=12\) years. At 9%, \(72/9=8\) years.

Worked interpretation. A savings account earning 6% interest compounded annually would take a little less than twelve years to double. The Rule of 72 is an estimate, not an exact equality. It is most useful as a quick sense check on growth.
Try it. Use the Rule of 72 to estimate the doubling time at 4%.
Check\(72/4=18\) years.

5. Loans: interest adds, payments subtract

A loan is also a changing balance, but here the goal is usually to reduce what is owed. Each period, interest is added to the remaining debt and then a payment is subtracted. A simplified balance rule is

\[B_{\text{next}}=B_{\text{current}}(1+r)-p,\]

where \(r\) is the interest rate per payment period and \(p\) is the payment for that period.

Worked example. A loan balance is $1,000. Monthly interest is 1% and the monthly payment is $100. Interest for the month is $10. The new balance is \(1000+10-100=\$910\). Next month's interest is based on $910, not $1,000.

Only the part of each payment beyond the interest reduces the principal. If the payment is too small even to cover interest, the balance can grow despite regular payments. This is one reason to examine more than the monthly payment when comparing loans.

Try it. A $500 balance incurs 2% interest for one period, followed by a $60 payment. What is the new balance?
Check\(500(1.02)-60=510-60=\$450\).

6. Observe a loan over its entire life

When choosing between loans, it helps to compare the total amount repaid, not only the monthly payment. A smaller payment may be easier to manage now, but a longer repayment period can mean paying much more interest in total.

To analyze a repayment plan, start from its actual balance, apply the correct periodic rate, subtract the payment, and repeat. A table makes the successive changes visible.
