Optimization: Choosing What Best Means

How can a business or community decide among several possible actions? We begin by identifying the goal, then modeling cost, revenue, and profit.

How to use this reading

The explanations below introduce the main ideas in this week's textbook chapters. Read them in order, working through the numerical examples before you reveal the brief checks. The original PDF chapters are kept in their existing form for additional examples, diagrams, and exercises.

1. Optimization begins with a question

Suppose a community group is organizing an event. It wants to serve as many people as possible, but food and labor have costs. It might want to maximize attendance, maximize money left for charity, or minimize expenses. These are not automatically the same goal.

Optimization means choosing a value that makes some quantity as large or as small as possible. The quantity we want to maximize or minimize is called the objective.

Before calculating anything, name the decision, the goal, and the limits. For instance, “What number of meals gives the largest profit?” is a different question from “How many meals can we prepare with today's supplies?”

Why the goal matters. A mathematically correct solution to the wrong objective can still be a poor real-world decision. Models help us clarify what we mean by “best.”

2. Cost functions: fixed costs and changing costs

A fixed cost is paid even if no items are produced, such as a rental fee. A variable cost depends on how many items are made, such as the cost of ingredients.

A simple linear cost model is

\[C(q)=F+cq,\]

where \(q\) is the number of items, \(F\) the fixed cost, and \(c\) the cost per additional item.

Worked example. A food booth costs $350 to set up and $4 per meal. For \(q\) meals, \(C(q)=350+4q\). At \(q=200\), the cost is \(350+4(200)=\$1150\). Even when \(q=0\), the fixed cost remains $350.

Graphically, \(F\) is the vertical intercept and \(c\) is the slope. Every additional item raises total cost by $4 in this model.

Try it. A club pays $250 per week plus $1.50 per loaf to operate a bakery. Write its cost function.
Check\(C(q)=250+1.5q\).

3. Revenue: money coming in

Revenue is the money received from sales. If each item sells for a fixed price \(p\), then

\[R(q)=pq.\]

Worked example. If a booth sells meals for $9 each, selling 200 meals brings in \(R(200)=9(200)=\$1800\). Revenue is not the same as profit because the booth must still pay its costs.

The selling price may sometimes depend on how many items customers are willing to buy. A business might lower its price to sell more. When a price \(p(q)\) varies with quantity, the revenue model becomes \(R(q)=q\,p(q)\).

An observer's question. Where did the price information come from? A simple model may assume every additional item sells, but real demand can change. Models should be tested against observations.

4. Profit and break-even points

Profit is revenue minus cost:

\[P(q)=R(q)-C(q).\]

When profit equals zero, the revenue just covers the cost. That is called a break-even point.

Worked example. Continue with the booth: \(R(q)=9q\), \(C(q)=350+4q\). Then \(P(q)=9q-(350+4q)=5q-350\). To break even, set \(0=5q-350\), so \(q=70\). Seventy meals exactly cover modeled costs; beyond 70, the linear model predicts positive profit.

Negative profit means a loss. It does not necessarily mean the activity has no value; a community group might be willing to subsidize an event for reasons other than profit.

Try it. At 100 meals, what profit does the booth model predict?
Check\(P(100)=5(100)-350=\$150\).

5. Marginal thinking: what changes if we do one more?

Instead of comparing a total with zero, sometimes we ask what happens when production increases by one unit. The marginal cost is the additional cost of one more unit, often estimated in an elementary model by \(C(q+1)-C(q)\).

Similarly, marginal revenue describes the additional revenue from one more sale, and marginal profit describes the change in profit.

Worked example. For \(C(q)=350+4q\), the cost of one additional meal is always $4. If revenue rises by $9 for that sale, marginal profit is $9 − $4 = $5. In a more realistic model, increasing quantity might force higher labor costs or lower prices, so the additional profit need not stay constant.

Comparing totals and comparing changes answer different questions. Both are valuable when deciding how much to produce.

6. Use a graph to compare choices

A graph can show cost, revenue, and profit as functions of quantity. The break-even point occurs where the cost and revenue graphs intersect. Between possible choices, look for a maximum or minimum that fits the question.

When revenue depends on a changing sale price, the profit graph may rise and then fall. The largest profit can occur at an intermediate number of sales rather than at the greatest possible quantity.

Simple table comparison. Suppose the predicted profit from selling 30, 40, 50, and 60 items is $90, $140, $150, and $120. Among these four options, the greatest predicted profit is $150 at 50 items. This table comparison identifies the best of the listed choices; it does not by itself prove that 50 is best among every possible quantity.
Try it. A plan earns slightly more profit but requires twice the unpaid volunteer time. Is the higher profit automatically the better decision? Explain what additional objective might matter.

7. The modeler chooses what counts as best

A business model may omit customer satisfaction, environmental impacts, employees' schedules, or community benefit. These are real aspects of a decision even when they are not reflected in a simple profit formula.

To improve a model, return to the context: What does the variable represent? Which quantities were measured? What goal are we optimizing? Which costs or limitations have we neglected?

Connection to Week 8. So far, we have described objectives such as maximizing profit. Next, we will add constraints—rules specifying which choices are actually available. The best possible choice must first be a feasible choice.
