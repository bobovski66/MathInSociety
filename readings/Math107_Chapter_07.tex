\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{What does C(40) ask us to do?}

Let \(q\) be the number of candles made \emph{and sold during one month}. In our first model, every candle made is sold. The letter q stands for quantity. It is the \emph{decision variable}: changing q describes a different plan.


A \emph{function} is a rule that assigns one output to each allowed input. We use \(C(q)\) for total cost, \(R(q)\) for revenue, and \(P(q)\) for profit, all in dollars for that same month. The notation \(C(40)\) means ``the cost when q is 40.'' It does not mean C multiplied by 40.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Substitute one choice into the rule},breakable]

If \(C(q)=180+4q\), replace q with 40:


\[C(40)=180+4(40)=180+160=340.\]


The input is 40 candles; the output is \$340 of modeled cost. The corresponding point on a cost graph is (40,340). At the same quantity, a different function can have a different output.


\end{tcolorbox}

For candles, sensible quantities are nonnegative whole numbers: 0, 1, 2, and so on. A graph may draw a continuous line between them to make the pattern visible. For services sold by the hour, fractional quantities may make sense. The story determines which inputs are allowed.

If q is candles per month, what do R(25) and P(25) mean? Are their units candles or dollars?
\end{tcolorbox}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Fixed cost is the starting amount; variable cost adds with quantity}

A \emph{fixed cost} does not change with the quantity in the period and range we are modeling. A stall fee is paid even if the stall sells nothing. A \emph{variable cost per unit} is the amount added for each item made: wax, a jar, and a wick might total \$4 per candle.


We write \(F\) for fixed cost and \(v\) for variable cost per item. The \emph{total} variable cost is \(vq\), so

\[C(q)=F+vq.\]

\par\medskip{\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}YYY@{}}\toprule
Symbol & Meaning in the candle model & Units\\
\midrule
q & Candles made and sold this month & candles\\
F = 180 & Stall and equipment cost for this month & dollars\\
v = 4 & Cost added per candle & dollars per candle\\
C(q) & Total modeled cost for the month & dollars\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}}\par\medskip


The units tell us why the formula works: dollars per candle multiplied by candles gives dollars. We can add that result to the fixed cost because both are money amounts.



\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.0in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week7/cost-components.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{The fixed portion stays at \$180. Each additional 20 candles adds \$80 of variable cost; total cost is not just the per-candle amount.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Identify the parts before calculating},breakable]

A food booth pays \$300 for equipment and \$50 for insurance. Ingredients cost \$3.50 per meal, and packaging costs \$0.50 per meal. Thus \(F=300+50=350\) dollars and \(v=3.50+0.50=4\) dollars per meal. Its model is \(C(q)=350+4q\), and \(C(200)=1150\) dollars.


\end{tcolorbox}

``Fixed'' is relative to the decision and time period. Renting a larger stall next month may change F. Today's model holds F constant while we compare quantities for the chosen stall. Also, zero production in this model still incurs the fixed expense; not opening at all may be a different option with different costs.

\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Revenue is not profit}

\emph{Revenue} is money received from sales. At a constant price \(p\) dollars per item, selling q items gives \(R(q)=pq\). For candles at \$10 each, \(R(q)=10q\). At 40 sales, revenue is \$400, but that money must also cover the \$340 cost.


\emph{Profit} is revenue minus the costs included in the model:

\[P(q)=R(q)-C(q)=pq-(F+vq)=(p-v)q-F.\]
The parentheses matter: subtract both the variable cost and the fixed cost. For the candles, \(P(q)=10q-(180+4q)=6q-180\). A negative result means a loss relative to the modeled expenses.



\par\medskip{\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}YYYY@{}}\toprule
Candles q & Cost C(q) & Revenue R(q) & Profit P(q)\\
\midrule
0 & \$180 & \$0 & $-$\$180\\
20 & \$260 & \$200 & $-$\$60\\
30 & \$300 & \$300 & \$0\\
40 & \$340 & \$400 & \$60\\
60 & \$420 & \$600 & \$180\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}}\par\medskip


At 20 candles, money still comes in: revenue is \$200. The loss of \$60 means that revenue falls \$60 short of the \$260 cost. It does not mean customers paid a negative amount. At 40 candles, the \$60 profit is what remains after the modeled costs.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={What is included in ``profit''?},breakable,colback=warm,colbacktitle=warm,coltitle=gold,colframe=gold!35]

If the model leaves out the maker's time, taxes, or delivery, the result has not accounted for those expenses. State what the cost model includes before treating the result as money available to spend. Our examples compare simplified plans, not complete business accounts.


\end{tcolorbox}

At 50 candles, find C(50), R(50), and P(50). Which result is money received before costs?
\end{tcolorbox}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Profit is a vertical difference at the same quantity}

The horizontal axis below measures candles for the month. The vertical axis measures dollars. To compare revenue and cost at 50 candles, stay above q=50 and read both heights. Revenue is \$500 and cost is \$380; their vertical difference is \$120.



\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.0in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week7/cost-revenue-gap.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{Use the same quantity for both heights. The intersection (30,\$300) says 30 candles bring in \$300 and cost \$300. The second coordinate is not profit.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip


The cost line starts at \$180 when q=0; this is its \emph{vertical intercept}. Revenue starts at zero. For every one-unit step to the right, cost rises \$4 and revenue rises \$10. Those changes are the slopes. A steeper revenue line eventually catches a cost line that started higher.



\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.0in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week7/profit-line.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{A profit graph uses the same quantity axis but plots R$-$C directly. Break-even is (30,\$0) here; it was (30,\$300) on the cost-and-revenue graph.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip


Below the profit graph's horizontal zero line are losses; above it are positive profits. Do not confuse ``the graph is rising'' with ``profit is already positive.'' From 0 to 20 candles, the loss shrinks, but the profit remains negative.

At q=20, the revenue graph is higher than at q=0. Does that prove the plan earns a profit?
\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Exact break-even and the first whole item avoiding a loss}

A plan \emph{breaks even} when revenue equals cost, so profit is exactly zero. For the candle model, solve \(6q-180=0\): add 180, then divide by 6, giving q=30 candles. This is an exact break-even quantity.


In general, if \(p>v\), each sale contributes the positive amount \(p-v\) toward the fixed expense. Setting profit to zero gives

\[(p-v)q-F=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q^*=\frac{F}{p-v}.\]
The star simply labels the threshold. Units provide another check: dollars divided by dollars per item gives items.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} A fractional threshold does not describe a whole sale},breakable]

At a craft fair, cards cost \$1.50 to make, sell for \$6, and require a \$48 table fee. Then \(P(q)=4.5q-48\) and \(q^*=48/4.5=10\tfrac23\). This is the exact crossing of the continuous lines.


At 10 cards, profit is $-$\$3. At 11, profit is \$1.50. Thus 11 is the smallest whole number avoiding a loss, but 11 is \emph{not} an exact break-even sale count. No whole number of cards gives profit exactly zero in this model.


\end{tcolorbox}


\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.0in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week7/whole-item-threshold.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{Rounding the threshold up identifies the first whole-item quantity with nonnegative profit. It does not move the exact zero of the function.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip


For an exact whole-number threshold such as 30 candles, 30 avoids a loss and 31 is the first whole count with \emph{strictly positive} profit. For a fractional threshold such as \(10\tfrac23\), 11 does both. Read whether the question asks for exact equality, nonnegative profit, or positive profit.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Before dividing, check the margin},breakable,colback=warm,colbacktitle=warm,coltitle=gold,colframe=gold!35]

If F is positive and \(p=v\), every sale only covers its own variable cost; the fixed expense remains uncovered. If \(p<v\), each sale adds to the loss. Neither case has a nonnegative break-even quantity. With F=0, q=0 breaks even; if p=v as well, every quantity breaks even.


\end{tcolorbox}


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 5},breakable]
A food truck has F=\$350, v=\$4, p=\$8. Find the exact threshold and the first whole-meal count avoiding a loss.
\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{One more sale changes profit by the margin}

\emph{Marginal} asks what changes when quantity increases by one. For our linear models, one additional candle raises cost by \$4, revenue by \$10, and profit by \$6. These are marginal cost, marginal revenue, and marginal profit.

\[P(q+1)-P(q)=p-v.\]
This difference is the same before and after break-even. Going from 20 to 21 candles changes profit from $-$\$60 to $-$\$54: an increase of \$6, while the business still has a loss. Going from 40 to 41 changes it from \$60 to \$66.



\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.0in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week7/marginal-steps.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{Marginal profit describes a change. Total profit describes the outcome at one quantity. A positive change can make a loss smaller without removing it.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip


This is Week 1's additive recursion in a new setting: start at \(P(0)=-180\), then repeatedly add 6. Cost starts at 180 and adds 4; revenue starts at 0 and adds 10. The input counts items rather than elapsed years.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} A larger change},breakable]

Increasing sales from 40 to 55 candles adds 15 sales, so profit increases by \(15(6)=90\) dollars. Check the totals: \(P(40)=60\) and \(P(55)=150\); their difference is \$90.


\end{tcolorbox}

The margin \$6 per candle is also called \emph{contribution per unit}. It is not the average total profit per candle: at 40 candles, that average is \(60/40=1.50\) dollars because the fixed cost still affects the total. In a nonlinear model, the extra profit from one more item may vary; constant margin is an assumption of this week's simple model.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 6},breakable]
A print shop has C(q)=500+2q and R(q)=6q. Find the marginal profit and compare P(124), P(125), and P(126).
\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Break-even is a threshold; an optimum is a best available outcome}

An \emph{objective function} assigns an outcome to each choice. To \emph{maximize} means to select the largest allowed value; to \emph{minimize} means to select the smallest. Here we often maximize P(q), but cost, access, time, or waste could be other objectives.


The candle profit \(6q-180\) rises with every sale. If the maker can produce and sell at most 60 candles this month, the allowed whole quantities are 0 through 60. The largest profit is \(P(60)=180\) dollars. Selling 30 breaks even, but 60 maximizes modeled profit within that limit.



\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.0in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week7/capacity-and-best.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{A best available plan can still have a loss. At capacity 25, even the best allowed quantity cannot cover the fixed cost. At capacity 60, the same model can earn a positive profit.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip


Without an upper limit or a demand model, a positive-margin linear profit has no largest value: choosing a larger q always gives a larger prediction. That is a sign that the model needs more context, not a recommendation to produce infinitely many candles.


On a fixed interval from 0 to a capacity, positive margin favors the largest quantity; negative margin favors zero; zero margin makes every quantity tie. These conclusions assume the fixed expense applies to all choices, including q=0. A separate ``do not open'' option may avoid that expense and needs its own comparison.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} A table compares only its listed options},breakable]

Suppose a model predicts profits \$90, \$140, \$150, and \$120 for quantities 30, 40, 50, and 60. Quantity 50 is best \emph{among those four options}. The table alone does not tell us the result at 49, 51, or every other possible quantity. A best-of-list claim needs fewer assumptions than a best-over-all-choices claim.


\end{tcolorbox}


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 7},breakable]
If the candle capacity is 24, what plan maximizes P(q)=6q$-$180, and is it profitable?
\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{Lower overhead and larger margin can favor different volumes}

Two shops sell the same product for \$20. Shop A has \$800 of fixed cost and \$8 per item of variable cost. Shop B has \$400 of fixed cost and \$12 per item. Their profit functions are \(P_A(q)=12q-800\) and \(P_B(q)=8q-400\).


At q=60, A loses \$80 while B earns \$80. At q=140, A earns \$880 while B earns \$720. B's smaller fixed expense helps at low volume; A's larger margin helps at high volume.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} Find the crossover},breakable]

Set the two profits equal: \(12q-800=8q-400\). Subtract 8q and add 800 to get \(4q=400\), hence q=100. Both profits are \$400 there.


This equality is a \emph{crossover between two plans}. It is not either shop's break-even point, which would require its own profit to equal zero. B breaks even at 50; A's continuous threshold is \(66\tfrac23\).


\end{tcolorbox}


\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.0in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week7/business-crossover.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{Equal profit between two businesses need not mean zero profit. Compare the same monthly quantity and the same cost categories before interpreting the lines.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip


A parameter change also has a recognizable effect. Increasing F shifts the cost line up and profit line down without changing their slopes. Increasing v lowers the margin. Increasing p raises the margin when other assumptions and the number sold stay fixed. These statements compare model outputs; they do not establish what demand will do.



\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.0in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week7/parameter-effects.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{An extra \$60 of fixed cost lowers profit by \$60 at every quantity. An extra \$2 of price raises modeled profit by \$2 times the quantity, if the number sold stays unchanged.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 8},breakable]
A business has P(q)=7q$-$420. Can you recover its fixed cost and margin? Can you recover its selling price and variable cost separately?
\end{tcolorbox}


\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{A higher price per item does not tell us how many items will sell}

A soap seller has F=\$200 and v=\$3. At a price of \$8 the margin is \$5; at \$12 it is \$9. If both plans sell exactly 40 bars, their profits are \$0 and \$160. That comparison answers a precise conditional question: \emph{what if the quantities are the same?}


Now suppose a separate, invented demand estimate predicts 70 sales at \$8 but only 30 at \$12. The profits become \(5(70)-200=150\) dollars and \(9(30)-200=70\) dollars. The lower-price plan now has the larger modeled profit.



\par\medskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=3.0in,keepaspectratio]{../assets/week7/price-and-demand.pdf}
\captionof*{figure}{The formulas did not contradict themselves. We changed the quantities used to compare the pricing plans. Demand estimates are additional assumptions, not consequences of the profit formula.}
\end{minipage}\par\medskip


If price depends on quantity, write it as \(p(q)\), so \(R(q)=q\,p(q)\). Revenue need not remain a straight line. We do not need a new formula to recognize the issue: changing price can change the number sold, and that relationship requires observations or a stated hypothetical model.


A smaller break-even threshold is measured in items, not in days. It does not mean a business will reach that threshold sooner in time. That would require information about the rate of sales. Likewise, a larger margin alone cannot determine total profit without a sales quantity and fixed cost.



\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Pause and try 9},breakable]
An \$8 plan earns \$150 by selling 70 bars. At a \$12 price with margin \$9 and fixed cost \$200, what whole-bar sales count would first exceed that profit?
\end{tcolorbox}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}\section{A useful recommendation names its assumptions}

For the original candle model, a complete conclusion is: ``If every candle made can be sold for \$10, costs stay at \$180 plus \$4 per candle, and capacity is 60 this month, choose 60 candles to maximize modeled profit at \$180.'' The quantity and the objective value are different parts of the answer.


Then identify what could change that conclusion. Unsold inventory separates production from sales. Limited labor can cap production. A delivery charge can add a new expense. A community group might accept a lower surplus to keep prices affordable. The arithmetic can compare outcomes after we state the goal and the available choices.


\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Example \textperiodcentered{} When production and sales are different},breakable]

Suppose 60 candles are made but only 40 sell. The cost is \(180+4(60)=420\) dollars, while revenue is \(10(40)=400\) dollars. Profit is $-$\$20. Substituting q=40 into the original profit rule would instead give \$60, because that rule assumes only 40 were made.


When production and sales differ, let \(n\) be candles made and \(s\) candles sold, with \(0\le s\le n\). Profit becomes \(P=10s-4n-180\), reflecting both quantities.


\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Name the choice, time period, and units.
\item State the goal and allowed quantities.
\item Build the cost and revenue rules, then subtract.
\item Check a threshold or compare the available choices.
\item Report a plan, its outcome, and an assumption worth checking.

\end{enumerate}

\begin{tcolorbox}[title={From Week 7 to Week 8},breakable,colback=warm,colbacktitle=warm,coltitle=gold,colframe=gold!35]

Week 8 will put two production quantities on a coordinate plane. Resource limits will define the feasible region, and an objective will give each feasible plan a score. The contribution p$-$v developed here becomes the coefficient attached to each product. If a fixed expense is the same for all plans, subtracting it changes profit values without changing which plan is best.


\end{tcolorbox}

Continue with the guided laboratory, apply the model to a food stand, and examine competing goals for an AI tutoring service. The pricing discussion asks you to communicate a comparison and its limits.

\section*{Further Worked Models}
\subsection*{Rebuild a linear cost rule from two observations}
Suppose a company's modeled cost is \$1,800 at 100 items and \$2,600 at 200 items, for the same month and operation. We are told to assume the cost relationship is linear.

The quantity increases by 100 while total cost increases by \$800. Therefore the variable cost is
\[
v=\frac{2600-1800}{200-100}=\$8\text{ per item}.
\]
Substitute into $C(q)=F+8q$: $1800=F+8(100)$, so $F=1000$. The cost rule is $C(q)=1000+8q$.

Check the second value: $C(200)=1000+1600=2600$. The fixed expense is the extrapolated cost at q=0 under the stated linear assumption. Two observations alone do not establish that a real cost rule is linear outside the range; the model supplied that assumption.

\subsection*{What a profit formula does not identify}
If $P(q)=7q-420$, then fixed cost is \$420 and the margin is \$7 per item. But the price and variable cost are not separately determined. Both $p=12,v=5$ and $p=15,v=8$ produce that same margin and profit rule.

The extra statement $P(80)=140$ would not distinguish them: it already follows from $7(80)-420=140$. To recover price and variable cost separately, we need new information such as the sale price or a cost observation.

\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Information and assumptions}]
An equation can answer the question its data determine. A missing price cannot be recovered merely by rearranging the difference $p-v$ in a second way. Recognizing missing information is part of solving a model correctly.
\end{tcolorbox}

\clearpage
\subsection*{Same break-even quantity, different profit at a chosen volume}
A food cart has $F=350$, $v=4$, and $p=9$ for one event. Its profit is $P(q)=5q-350$, exact break-even is 70 meals, and profit at 100 sales is \$150.

Compare two changes separately. Option A raises price to \$11 without changing the other costs. Option B reduces fixed cost to \$250 while keeping the original price.

{\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}YYYY@{}}\toprule
Plan & Profit rule & Exact threshold & Profit at 100 sales\\\midrule
Original & $5q-350$ & 70 & \$150\\
A: price \$11 & $7q-350$ & 50 & \$350\\
B: fixed cost \$250 & $5q-250$ & 50 & \$250\\\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

Both changes give a 50-meal threshold, but A has a larger profit at 100 sales because it has a larger margin. The threshold alone does not describe the complete comparison. We also held the sales count fixed; a price increase or a smaller location could change actual sales.

\subsection*{An additional observation can change the recommendation}
Suppose a separate, invented sales estimate predicts 70 meals under A and 100 under B. Then A's profit is $7(70)-350=140$, while B's remains \$250. Under those estimates, B has the larger profit.

The estimates do not follow from the algebra. They are additional inputs that can be tested against observations. The calculation makes the consequences of each estimate visible.

\begin{tcolorbox}[title={A conclusion with its conditions}]
``At 100 sales under either plan, A gives \$100 more profit than B'' is supported by the first comparison. ``A is always the better real business decision'' is a stronger claim that the fixed-sales model does not establish.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection*{Chapter review: solutions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item $C(q)=800+3q$, $R(q)=8q$, and $P(q)=5q-800$. Exact break-even is 160 pamphlets. For q-values 0, 80, 160, 200, 240, costs are 800, 1040, 1280, 1400, 1520; revenues are 0, 640, 1280, 1600, 1920; profits are $-800,-400,0,200,400$. At 240 sales, \$400 remains after the costs included in this model.
\item $C=600+7q$, $R=25q$, $P=18q-600$; margin is \$18 per car. The exact crossing is $600/18=33\tfrac13$, so 34 cars first avoid a loss. For profit at least \$360, solve $18q-600\geq360$, obtaining $q\geq53\tfrac13$. The first whole count is 54, with P(54)=372; P(53)=354 is insufficient.
\item Margin is 9 and threshold is 100. P(150)=450. Cost begins at (0,900) and has slope 12 dollars per item; revenue begins at (0,0) and has slope 21. Their intersection is (100,2100). On the profit graph, break-even is (100,0); the zero is profit, not the common cost and revenue.
\item $P(q)=60q-320$. Exact break-even is $320/60=16/3$ hours. For \$1,000 profit, solve $60q-320=1000$, obtaining q=22 billed hours. The modeled profit includes the listed time cost; costs not named are not automatically included.
\item Originally P=9q\ensuremath{-}540, the threshold is 60 and P(80)=180. At the lower price, P=6q\ensuremath{-}540, the threshold is 90 and P(80)=\ensuremath{-}60. The threshold increases by 30 items. The fixed-sales comparison does not predict whether lower price attracts enough additional sales; a demand observation or explicit estimate is needed.
\item $P_A=5q-400$, $P_B=3q-200$. Their exact thresholds are 80 and $66\tfrac23$. Equality gives $2q=200$, so q=100 and both profits are \$100. At 60, A gives \ensuremath{-}\$100 and B \ensuremath{-}\$20, so B has the smaller loss. At 140, A gives \$300 and B \$220, so A is higher. A has a larger fixed expense but a larger margin; the crossover is not a zero-profit crossing.
\item $C(0)=F$ and $C(q+1)=C(q)+v$. $R(0)=0$ and $R(q+1)=R(q)+p$. $P(0)=-F$ and $P(q+1)=P(q)+(p-v)$. The input counts units made and sold in one period; it compares possible volumes rather than automatically tracking days or months.
\item The cost change is \$800 for 100 extra items, so v=8. Then F=1800\ensuremath{-}8(100)=1000 and C=1000+8q. Substitution gives 1800 and 2600 at the stated quantities. From P=7q\ensuremath{-}420, F=420 and p\ensuremath{-}v=7; p and v separately require additional information.
\item $P=4.5q-48$, with exact threshold $32/3=10\tfrac23$. The first whole count avoiding loss is 11. P(10)=\ensuremath{-}3, P(11)=1.50, and P(12)=6 dollars. No whole count makes P exactly zero because the unique zero is not an integer.
\newpage
\item (a) P=\ensuremath{-}100 at every quantity, so none break even. (b) P=\ensuremath{-}q\ensuremath{-}100 is negative for every nonnegative q, so none break even. (c) P=0 at every nonnegative q, so every quantity breaks even. (d) P=\ensuremath{-}q, so only q=0 breaks even; positive quantities lose money.
\item Positive margin makes the largest allowed quantity best: q=25, P=\ensuremath{-}30. The exact threshold q=30 is beyond capacity. Under the original fixed-cost model even the best plan loses \$30. The explicitly separate no-opening option gives profit 0 if it avoids all costs, and would outperform that loss. We must not silently remove a fixed expense from one of the original plans.
\item Quantity 50 gives the largest profit among the four listed options. The table does not determine profit at 49, 51, or unlisted choices, so it cannot establish a global maximum. We need more allowed-choice values or a justified function and domain.
\item At \$10 with 60 sales, P=6(60)\ensuremath{-}180=180. At \$12 with 35 sales, P=8(35)\ensuremath{-}180=100. The lower-price plan is higher under these particular sales assumptions. To exceed 180 at the higher price, $8q-180>180$, so q>45. At least 46 whole sales are needed; 45 only ties.
\item Cost is 240+3.5(80)=520, revenue 9.5(60)=570, profit \$50. The one-count formula at 60 gives \$120 because it charges variable cost for only 60 prepared meals. The 20 additional prepared but unsold meals cost 20(3.5)=70 dollars, explaining the difference. Sales do not erase production costs.

The two maximizing goals coincide here because margin is positive and all paid sessions have the same modeled revenue and cost. A minimum of 5001 with capacity 5000 is infeasible. Learning, accuracy, and affordability need separate evidence and criteria.
\item At \$20, margin is 12 and exact break-even is 100 attendees. At \$15, margin is 7 and the crossing is $1200/7=171\tfrac37$; 172 first avoid a loss. At 172, cost is 1200+8(172)=2576, revenue is 15(172)=2580, and profit is \$4. Access, mission, attendance, and service quality may also matter; the formula does not choose their priority.
