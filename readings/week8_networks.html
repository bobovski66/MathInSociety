Module 8 · Graph Theory and Networks

How connections shape a community

A practical introduction to networks, paths, vulnerable connections, and information spreading.

The question for this week: How can we tell which connections in a community network matter most, and where an additional link would make a difference?

A county has seven locations that share emergency information through established two-way contacts. Our drawing records the contacts; it is not a map of real distances.

A: coordinator · B: library · C: clinic · D: school · E: shelter · F: north block · G: community center

1. Who can contact whom directly?

Each dot is a node (a vertex). Each line is an edge (a direct relationship). Mathematicians call this collection a graph. It shows connections between things, rather than a quantity changing on a coordinate plane.

Our connections are AB, AC, BC, BD, CD, DE, EF, EG, and FG. A connection works both ways. A and D may be close on the page, but they have no direct edge. The drawing's line lengths do not tell us how far apart places are.

Worked example: count direct contacts

D connects directly to B, C, and E, so its degree is 3. The degrees of A through G are 2, 3, 3, 3, 3, 2, 2. Their sum is 18. Why not nine? Every edge has two ends, so counting the degree at every location counts each edge twice. This is the handshake rule.

Your turn: A pantry P has direct partnerships with library L and school S. The school also partners with community fridge F, and F partners with senior hub H; L also partners with S. The edges are PL, PS, LS, SF, FH. Who has degree 3? Who has degree 1? Think about what that means for access.

2. How do you get from one location to another?

A path follows a chain of edges. A–B–D–E–G is a valid route from A to G. It visits five locations but uses four edges. Another four-edge path is A–C–D–E–G. The fewest-edge route is called a shortest path.

Worked example: reachability

A and G are connected even though they are not direct neighbors: the four-edge path allows a message to be relayed. The whole graph is connected, because every pair of nodes has some path between them.

In our simple network, distance means the number of links. For walking or driving, we might prefer travel time. Suppose a station-to-hospital trip through the clinic uses two walkways taking 8 and 9 minutes: 17 minutes altogether. A different route uses three walkways taking 4, 3, and 5 minutes: 12 minutes. More links can mean less time. The minutes are weights attached to the paths.

Your turn: Find a valid B-to-F path and count its links. Why is A–D–G not valid? How might accessibility change the recommendation for an actual hospital walk?

3. What happens when a connection fails?

Imagine that the school-to-shelter contact D–E goes down. The network separates into two internally connected groups. A group that has routes within it but no route to other groups is a connected component.

The dashed D–E connection has failed. The remaining groups are {A, B, C, D} and {E, F, G}.

D–E is a bridge: removing it increases the number of components. By contrast, B–C is not a bridge. If that link fails, B can still reach C through A or D. The difference is visible when you actually trace alternate paths.

Your turn: In the food-sharing network PL, PS, LS, SF, FH, what happens to H when the F–H partnership disappears? Is L–S also a bridge? Show an alternate route if one exists.

4. Where should we add one connection?

The county can add only one direct contact. Compare adding A–D with adding D–F. Both increase the count from nine edges to ten, but they accomplish different things.

Add A–D: A can reach D in one step, but D–E remains the only link across the two regions.

Add D–F: A second connection between regions makes the network more resilient to loss of D–E.

If D–E fails after adding D–F, A can still reach G using A–B–D–F–G. An alternate route is called redundancy. But the new D–F link does not protect against the school D itself failing; both cross-region links still rely on D.

Big lesson: Two networks can have exactly the same number of links yet behave differently. The arrangement of connections matters. A good decision also depends on the objective: faster direct contact, surviving failures, or something else.
Your turn: If the goal is to reach D from A in one step, which proposal wins? If the goal is to survive failure of D–E, which wins? Explain why the answers differ.

5. How does an alert spread through the network?

Return to the original nine links. Only A is informed at round 0. During each round, informed locations send to all direct neighbors. Newly informed locations can send beginning in the next round. Under this simple rule, every available transmission succeeds.

The first round when a location learns the alert matches its shortest-path distance from A. If D–E fails with no alternate link, the alert never reaches E, F, or G under these assumptions. Waiting longer cannot create a missing connection.

Your turn: At round 2, how many new locations are informed and how many total? Why shouldn't we interpret a model round as a real-world minute?

6. Use the same method in your world

The same diagrams can describe food-sharing partnerships, school support groups, bus routes, or computers exchanging information. You must say what a line means. A direct friendship is not the same kind of connection as a road or a referral.

Some situations need an extra rule. A message may be forwarded immediately, while a person may decide to join a group only after several contacts have joined. The graph describes possible connections; an update rule describes what happens over time. That's the bridge to our next module on computation and complex systems.

Five-step routine: (1) Name the nodes and edges. (2) State the question. (3) Trace or count the relevant connections. (4) Compare one change. (5) Explain a limitation.
