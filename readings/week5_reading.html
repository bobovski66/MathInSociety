Cybernetics: Recursion, Feedback, and Oscillation

How can a system that is trying to reach a target begin to swing back and forth? We can find out using the sequences you learned to build in Module 1.

1. Return to recursion: yesterday determines today

In Module 1, you learned that a recursive sequence begins with a starting value and uses a rule to find the next value. If \(a_0=4\) and \(a_{n+1}=a_n+3\), the sequence is \(4,7,10,13,\ldots\). Each new number depends on one that came before it.

The rule tells us how one step leads to the next. This is exactly the perspective we need for feedback. A thermometer reads the current temperature; a heater responds; that action changes the temperature that will be read on the following step.

Our example for the week. A greenhouse has a target temperature of \(70^\circ\mathrm F\). It begins at \(64^\circ\mathrm F\). Each hour, its controller adjusts the temperature by half of the difference from the target. We will represent the temperature after step \(n\) by \(x_n\). The subscript \(n\) is a step number, not an exponent.
Pause and try. The rule \(a_{n+1}=a_n-2\) begins with \(a_0=10\). List \(a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3\).
Check your sequence\(10,8,6,4\). Every step uses the value immediately before it.

2. Turn a sequence into a feedback loop

Our greenhouse is different from the sequence that simply subtracts 2. A correction depends on how far the current temperature is from \(70^\circ\mathrm F\). That difference is the control error.

\[\text{error}=70-x_n,\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n+0.5(70-x_n).\]

We read the formula from left to right: start with the current temperature; find the distance from the target; take half that distance; add the correction. If the temperature is above the target, the error is negative, so the correction lowers it.

First two steps. At \(x_0=64\), the error is \(70-64=6\). Half the error is \(3\), so \(x_1=64+3=67\). The next error is \(70-67=3\). Half of that is \(1.5\), so \(x_2=67+1.5=68.5\).

3. What changes when information arrives one step late?

Now imagine the thermometer takes one full step to deliver a report. At step \(n\), the controller has the previous step's reading, not the current one. The heater changes the current temperature using that older information.

\[\boxed{x_{n+1}=x_n+0.5(70-x_{n-1})}\]

Notice both subscripts: \(x_n\) is the temperature now, while \(x_{n-1}\) is the older temperature used to decide the correction. We cannot exchange them. To start the delayed rule, we need two values. Assume the temperature was \(64^\circ\mathrm F\) just before we began, so \(x_{-1}=x_0=64\).

First three delayed steps. At step 0, the old reading is 64, so \(x_1=64+0.5(70-64)=67\). At step 1, the old reading is still 64, even though the greenhouse is now 67. So \(x_2=67+0.5(70-64)=70\). At step 2, the older reading is 67. The heater still asks for a positive correction of \(0.5(70-67)=1.5\). Hence \(x_3=70+1.5=71.5\). We have passed the target!

4. A graph makes the difference visible

Tables show exactly how each new value is calculated. Graphs show the behavior of the whole sequence. Step numbers go on the horizontal axis; temperature goes on the vertical axis. The dashed horizontal line marks our target of 70°F.

With immediate feedback, the green sequence approaches the target from below. With delayed feedback, the red sequence goes above 70, comes back down, crosses below 70, and continues with smaller swings. That back-and-forth behavior is an oscillation. Here the oscillation is damped: its departures from 70 eventually become smaller.

Our main discovery: A controller can try to correct an error and still overshoot because its information arrives too late. The delay changes the sequence and therefore changes its long-term behavior.

5. What actually causes the oscillation?

Imagine steering a bicycle toward the middle of a path, but seeing your position only as it was a moment earlier. You may continue steering left after you have already reached the middle. When the delayed report catches up, you steer right; then you may keep steering right for too long. Repeated overcorrection can produce a side-to-side motion.

The greenhouse behaves similarly. The action changes the state before the controller learns about the change. The next decision is made from a view of the past. The result can be alternating corrections, even though every correction was meant to push toward the target.

Delay can produce oscillation, but not every delay produces it and oscillation need not come from delay. The amount of correction and the way the system responds also matter. Our numerical example is designed to show an oscillation created by adding a single step of delay while holding everything else fixed.

Predict. In the delayed example, at step 4 the present temperature is 71.5 and the old reading is also 71.5. Is the next correction positive or negative?
CheckNegative. It is \(0.5(70-71.5)=-0.75\), so the next temperature is 70.75.

6. Response strength can also cause a swing

Suppose there is no delay, but we use a larger response strength. The immediate rule is now \(x_{n+1}=x_n+1.5(70-x_n)\). Starting from 64, the first correction is \(1.5(6)=9\), taking the temperature to 73. The next correction is \(1.5(70-73)=-4.5\), so the temperature falls to 68.5.

The sequence begins \(64,73,68.5,70.75,\ldots\). It oscillates because the controller reacts too strongly, not because the measurement is old. This is an important comparison with our delayed example, where even the gentle strength \(0.5\) produced an oscillation.

Compare two mechanisms: Strong immediate feedback can overshoot because the correction is larger than the remaining error. Delayed feedback can overshoot because a formerly appropriate correction continues after the situation has changed. In both cases, sequences and graphs help us see what happens.

7. Where statistics enters the loop

Last week's mean, standard deviation, normal distribution, and z-score help us assess the measurements entering a feedback loop. A sensor reading can fluctuate even if the temperature does not change. If a stable sensor has mean 68°F and standard deviation 2°F, a 72°F reading has z-score \((72-68)/2=2\). It is unusual but not impossible.

To reduce random measurement noise, a system might average several readings. But collecting more observations can take time, introducing delay. That gives us a genuine tradeoff: more precise information versus information that arrives sooner.

Two errors, two questions. Measurement error is how far a reading is from the actual temperature. Control error is target minus the reading or estimate used for the decision. These are different. Before changing the greenhouse, we should ask both how far it appears to be from the target and whether the measurement is trustworthy.

The statistical connection matters because delay and noise have different effects: a delayed reading may be accurate about an earlier time, while a noisy reading may be inaccurate even when it is current.

8. Cybernetics: when information changes what happens next

So far we have studied two temperature sequences. They illustrate a larger subject called cybernetics: the study of communication, feedback, and control in machines, living things, and social systems. Cybernetics asks how a system uses information about what has happened to influence what happens next.

A familiar control system has several distinct parts. The state is the condition that can change (room temperature). A sensor measures that state (thermometer). The reference is the desired value (70°F). A controller compares the reading with the reference and decides what to do. An actuator carries out the action (heater or cooling fan). The new temperature affects the next reading, completing a feedback loop.

Trace the complete loop. The thermometer reports 64°F. The thermostat compares that reading with its reference of 70°F and calculates an error of +6°F. Its rule calls for heating. The greenhouse warms, so the next reading is different. The measurement is not merely recorded; it changes what the controller does, and what the controller does changes the next measurement.

The crucial idea is sometimes called circular causation. The situation influences the action, and the action influences the situation. That is different from following a fixed schedule that runs without checking the outcome.

Try it. A sprinkler runs at exactly 6 a.m. every day, rain or shine. Another sprinkler turns on only when a soil sensor reports dry ground. Which is using feedback, and what measurement closes the loop?
CheckThe soil-sensor sprinkler uses feedback because the observed moisture affects its action. The timed sprinkler follows a schedule, whether or not the ground needs water.

9. Three important kinds of feedback behavior

Corrective (negative) feedback: resist a change

Our greenhouse controller uses negative feedback, also called corrective feedback. When the temperature falls below the target, the controller heats; when it rises above the target, it cools. Negative means that the loop opposes a departure, not that the outcome is bad. A successful corrective loop can maintain a reasonably steady condition, sometimes called regulation or homeostasis.

Amplifying (positive) feedback: reinforce a change

Some loops make an increase more likely to create another increase. Think of a rumor: the more people hear it, the more people can pass it along. If the number of recipients increases by 20% each round, an idealized rule is \(v_{n+1}=1.2v_n\). Starting at 100 gives \(100,120,144,172.8,\ldots\). This is positive or amplifying feedback: the process reinforces a departure rather than correcting it. Positive does not automatically mean beneficial. Real spreading processes eventually face limits.

Adaptive feedback: change the rule itself

A controller can also learn. Suppose a student answers practice problems, sees which answers were incorrect, changes how they study, and tries again. The information returned after one attempt changes the next attempt. Some machine-learning systems operate on a related principle: make a prediction, calculate an error, adjust a parameter, and repeat.

A tiny learning sequence. Let the prediction be \(\widehat y=2w_n\), with target 6. Starting at \(w_0=1\), the first prediction is 2 and the error is 4. Under the simple update \(w_{n+1}=w_n+0.25(6-2w_n)\), the new weight is \(w_1=2\). Then the next prediction is 4. Another update gives \(w_2=2.5\), and prediction 5. Learning is visible as a sequence of updated parameters.
Classify each loop. A thermostat maintains room temperature. A video is reshared more often as it becomes popular. A robot adjusts its future movements after missing a target. Which is corrective, amplifying, and adaptive?
CheckThermostat: corrective. Reshared video: amplifying. Robot changing its future behavior from errors: adaptive.

Notice how these ideas connect to the earlier graph. Even corrective feedback can oscillate if the controller's information is delayed or its response is too strong. The goal of regulation does not guarantee a perfectly steady result.

10. Cybernetics depends on information and communication

Information must travel from the part of the system being measured to the part that decides what to do. In the greenhouse, the thermometer sends a signal to the thermostat. The signal might be accurate and prompt, accurate but late, or current but noisy. Each case can produce a different decision.

A useful feedback loop must therefore answer three questions: What is measured? How is that measurement used to choose an action? When does the result of the action become available for the next decision? The third question brings us back to our central experiment with delayed sequences.

One mathematical framework, several applications. The state of a system produces information; that information influences an action; the action produces a new state. The sequence \(x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots\) lets us study this cycle one step at a time. Cybernetics tells us what the steps mean.

11. Extensions: further examples of cybernetic systems

The same sequential viewpoint applies far beyond a thermostat. A message spreading from one group to another might follow \(v_{n+1}=1.2v_n\): each round has 20% more exposure than the previous one. This is an example of amplifying feedback, in which a change creates conditions for further change.

Learning can also be understood recursively. A simple predictor makes an estimate, compares it with a desired result, and changes an adjustable number before its next attempt. In the AI application, a small model uses a sequence of weights \(w_0,w_1,w_2,\ldots\). Each weight depends on a measured prediction error from the previous attempt.

These applications are different, but they share the same mathematical pattern: observe a state, use a rule, obtain a new state, and repeat.

When you encounter a changing system, begin by asking: What is \(x_n\)? What rule determines \(x_{n+1}\)? Which earlier observations does that rule use? What does the sequence look like on a graph?
