Chapter 5 — From Statistical Observation to Feedback and Control

Math in Society · Week 5 · Reading

What you will learn

You already know how to summarize measurements with a mean and standard deviation. Now you will discover how those measurements can guide decisions—and why variation and uncertainty matter when we act.

Work through the examples slowly. The questions after each section are opportunities to explain the mathematics in your own words, not speed tests.

1. From statistics to cybernetics

Last week, we described collections of measurements. We used the mean to describe their center, standard deviation to describe their spread, and normal distributions to describe a common pattern of variation. We also used z-scores to ask whether a measurement was ordinary or unusual.

This week, we ask a new question: What happens when someone uses those measurements to make a decision? When the decision changes the situation and the situation is measured again, we have a feedback loop.

Community greenhouse. Volunteers want the greenhouse near 70°F. A thermometer reports 66°F, so the heater turns on. Later, the volunteers take another reading and decide whether more heat is needed. The second measurement matters because of the first action.

The cycle is observe → compare → decide → act → observe again. A thermostat, a driver, and a student changing study habits all use versions of this pattern.

Try it. In the greenhouse, what is measured? What is the target? What action changes the temperature?

2. A measurement is not the same as the truth

A thermometer does not give us direct access to the exact temperature. It gives us a reading. Real readings can vary because of the sensor, its position, air currents, rounding, or actual changes in the greenhouse.

Suppose five readings, taken close together while conditions are fairly steady, are 68, 70, 69, 71, and 67°F. Their mean is

\[\bar x=\frac{68+70+69+71+67}{5}=69.\]

We use 69°F as a useful summary of the readings. No single measurement is guaranteed to represent the entire greenhouse exactly.

Two different kinds of difference. If the target is 70°F and the estimated current temperature is 69°F, the control error is 70 − 69 = 1°F. Measurement error means the difference between a sensor reading and the temperature it is trying to measure. We may not know that second difference exactly.

These two kinds of error must not be confused. The control error tells us what correction we want. Measurement uncertainty tells us how much trust to place in the observation used to choose that correction.

Try it. A sensor reports 73°F while a trustworthy reference thermometer reads 71°F. What is the sensor's measurement error? If the desired temperature is 70°F, what is the control error based on the reference thermometer?

3. Normal distributions explain false alarms

Remember the bell-shaped normal distribution. Values near the mean are common; values many standard deviations away are less common. We can use that knowledge when deciding whether to respond to a surprising reading.

Imagine that a stable sensor produces readings that are approximately normal, with mean 68°F and standard deviation 2°F. A reading of 72°F is

\[z=\frac{72-68}{2}=2\]

standard deviations above its mean. In the normal model, about 95% of readings lie within two standard deviations of the mean, and about 5% lie outside that interval altogether. A reading of 72°F is unusual, but not impossible.

Why does this matter? If we respond strongly to every unusual reading, we may change the heater because of sensor noise rather than a meaningful temperature change. The decision itself can create a new problem.

A single unusual reading is a reason to pay attention, not automatically proof that the underlying system changed. If we can take several independent, unbiased measurements of a stable condition, their average is generally less variable than one reading.

For example, if individual sensor errors have standard deviation 2°F, the average of four independent errors has standard deviation \(2/\sqrt4=1\)°F. Averaging reduces random variation, but waiting to collect readings may also delay a needed action.

Try it. Is a reading two standard deviations above the mean necessarily a broken sensor? Would averaging several readings make you more or less sensitive to a single unusual reading?

4. An elementary feedback rule

We now describe what the system does with a measurement. Let r be the target, x the current state, and k the fraction of the observed difference that we correct in one step:

\[\text{error}=r-x,\qquad \text{new state}=x+k(r-x).\]

This is an idealized model: it assumes the adjustment changes the state by exactly the computed amount. Real greenhouse equipment responds more gradually and is affected by weather and other disturbances.

A gentle correction. Start at 64°F, aim for 70°F, and let k = 0.5. First error: 70 − 64 = 6. Correct half of that: 0.5 × 6 = 3. New state: 64 + 3 = 67°F. Repeat: 70 − 67 = 3, so the next correction is 1.5 and the next state is 68.5°F.

5. A stronger response is not always better

When the response is gentle, the state may approach its target slowly. When the response is too strong, the state may jump beyond the target and then jump back. This back-and-forth movement is an oscillation.

With target 70°F, starting temperature 64°F, and k = 1.5, the first update gives 73°F. The next error is −3°F, so the next update gives 68.5°F. The system crosses the target repeatedly but the jumps shrink.

Now add uncertain measurements. Suppose the actual temperature is near 70°F but the sensor sometimes reports 67°F or 73°F. A controller that reacts forcefully to each reading can cause unnecessary swings. The system is no longer correcting only the environment: it is also responding to the noise in its information.

Delayed feedback creates another problem. If the greenhouse is now 74°F but the controller receives an older report of 68°F, the old report asks for heat even though the greenhouse is already above the target.

Try it. Which might cause a needless correction: an unusually high sensor reading, an old reading, or both? Explain without using a formula.

6. Seeing patterns: a simple control chart

In manufacturing, public health, and other settings, people often collect a sequence of measurements and plot them over time. This is called a control chart. Its purpose is to help distinguish routine variation from evidence that a process may have changed.

Suppose a packaging machine fills bags with an average of 100 g and a standard deviation of 2 g. If the individual weights are approximately normal, roughly 68% should be between 98 and 102 g, roughly 95% between 96 and 104 g, and roughly 99.7% between 94 and 106 g.

These are reference intervals based on the Empirical Rule. A weight beyond 94–106 g is uncommon in this model, so it could prompt an investigation. One unusual value is not proof that the machine needs adjustment. A sustained shift in several observations might be more convincing.

Important distinction. Checking for an unusual measurement and deciding how to change a machine are two different steps. The first is a statistical judgment; the second is a control decision.
Try it. A bag weighs 105 g. Is this within three standard deviations of 100 g? What would you want to know before changing the machine?

7. When feedback amplifies change

Not every feedback loop pushes toward a target. Sometimes a change encourages more change in the same direction. This is amplifying feedback, or positive feedback. Positive does not mean good.

For example, a message might reach 100 people today and 20% more people in each later round. The numbers are 100, 120, 144, and so on. An increase in attention produces conditions for even more attention.

The mathematics is familiar from percentage growth: \(\text{new amount}=1.20\times\text{old amount}\). The connection to cybernetics comes from the mechanism: more attention makes more sharing possible, and the result feeds back into the next round.

Try it. Give an example of feedback that corrects a difference and another that amplifies a change.

8. Learning from repeated observations

A student takes a practice quiz, studies the mistakes, and tries again. This is a learning loop: an attempt produces information about an outcome, and the information changes the next attempt.

A computer model can also change in response to measured error. Imagine a toy prediction rule \(\widehat y=2w\), where w is adjustable. The desired prediction is 6. Begin with w = 1. The model predicts 2, leaving error 6 − 2 = 4. Under the simple rule \(w_{new}=w+0.25\,\text{error}\), the new weight is 2 and the next prediction is 4. A later update moves it closer to 6.

This example uses exact, noiseless targets. With real sample data, measurements and outcomes vary. Updating too strongly after each surprising result can make the model chase noise, just as a thermostat can chase a faulty thermometer reading.

Neural networks use many adjustable weights. A method called backpropagation helps determine how each weight contributes to prediction error. You do not need to compute that process here; the important idea is that repeated observations guide later adjustments.

Try it. Explain in one sentence how a practice quiz and a thermostat share a feedback structure. In what way is the information they receive imperfect?

9. Putting the whole course together

Statistics helps us understand what a measurement means and how much it may vary. Cybernetics studies how measurements affect decisions and how those decisions change the next observations. We need both ideas whenever we manage a changing situation.

Before acting, ask: What is the target? What did we actually measure? Could the reading be unusual because of ordinary variation? What action will we take? When will we measure again? How will we know whether the action helped?

The next chapter will develop mathematical descriptions of how quantities change over time. Feedback gives us a reason to study those changing quantities: their future behavior depends on what the system does with information from the past.
