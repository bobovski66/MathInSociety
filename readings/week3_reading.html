Basic Statistics: Center, Spread, and Comparison

Two data sets can have the same typical value and still behave very differently. Statistics provides language for both their center and their variation.

1. Why summarize a distribution?

Last week, we collected observations and arranged them in tables or graphs. Now suppose two neighborhoods have the same average commute time. Does that guarantee commuters in those neighborhoods have similar experiences? Not at all. One neighborhood might have commute times clustered tightly around the average; the other might have very short and very long trips.

A useful numerical summary should tell us two things: where the distribution is centered and how much its observations vary. We call the first idea center and the second spread.

No single number can communicate everything. We use the mean or median together with a description of variability and, when possible, a graph.

2. The mean: sharing the total equally

The mean is what each value would be if the total were redistributed equally. Add the values, then divide by the number of observations:

\[\bar x=\frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n}{n}.\]

Worked example. Five students spend \(2,3,3,5,7\) hours studying for a test. Their total is \(20\) hours, so the mean is \(20/5=4\) hours. The mean need not be a value anyone actually reported: no student in this example studied exactly four hours.

The mean also has a balance-point interpretation: values below the mean and values above it balance when their signed deviations are added. This is one reason it is useful in mathematical models.

Try it. Find the mean of \(4,5,5,6\).
Check\((4+5+5+6)/4=5\).

3. The median and mode: different questions about typical

The median is the middle value after the observations are put in order. If there is an even number of observations, average the two middle values. The mode is the value appearing most often. A set can have more than one mode, or no unique mode.

Worked example. Consider annual salaries (in thousands of dollars): \(38,42,45,47,50,310\). The mean is \(532/6\approx 88.7\), or about $88,700. The median is \((45+47)/2=46\), or $46,000. One unusually high salary pulls the mean upward while leaving the middle pair unchanged.

For distributions with substantial outliers or a long tail, the median can describe a typical case more meaningfully than the mean. However, the mean still answers the important question “What is the total divided equally?”

Try it. A dataset is \(1,2,2,7,18\). What are its median and mode?
CheckThe median is \(2\), and the mode is also \(2\).

4. Range: a first measurement of spread

The range is the largest value minus the smallest value. It tells us the full distance from one extreme to the other.

Worked comparison. Group A has observations \(8,9,10,11,12\). Group B has \(2,6,10,14,18\). Both have mean \(10\). Group A has range \(12-8=4\), while Group B has range \(18-2=16\). The identical means hide a major difference in spread.

Because range uses only the minimum and maximum, one extreme observation can change it dramatically. To account for all the observations, we need a more informative measurement of spread.

5. Sample standard deviation: typical distance from the mean

Sample standard deviation, written \(s\), measures how far values tend to lie from the mean. It starts with the differences \(x_i-\bar x\), called deviations. We square them so that positive and negative deviations do not cancel. Then we divide their sum by \(n-1\) and take a square root.

\[s=\sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_i-\bar x)^2}{n-1}}.\]

Worked example, step by step. For observations \(2,4,6\): the mean is \(4\). The deviations are \(-2,0,2\). Their squares are \(4,0,4\), which total \(8\). Since \(n=3\), the sample variance is \(8/(3-1)=4\), and the sample standard deviation is \(\sqrt4=2\).

A larger standard deviation means observations are more spread out in the units of the original data. The number is not a score for how “good” a dataset is; it is a description of variation.

Try it. For data \(3,3,3\), what is the sample standard deviation?
CheckIt is \(0\). Every value equals the mean, so all deviations are zero.

6. Use center and spread together

Consider two classes that both average 80 on a quiz. If almost everyone in one class scores between 78 and 82, its scores have low spread. If many students in the other class score near 60 or 100, its spread is much higher. “The average was 80” does not tell the whole story.

When comparing data, look for shape as well: symmetry, skewness, clusters, gaps, and unusual values. Histograms can reveal patterns that are invisible in a table of just the mean and standard deviation.

Connection to Week 4. A normal distribution is one especially useful pattern of shape. Its mean identifies the center, while its standard deviation determines the width of the familiar bell curve.
