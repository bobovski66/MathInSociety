Optimization Under Constraints: Geometry to Decisions

Some choices are desirable but impossible. We first draw the region of allowable choices, then use linear programming to find the best option inside that region.

How to use this reading

1. First describe what is allowed

Suppose a school club makes two kinds of snack packs. Each pack takes time and supplies. Even if producing hundreds would be profitable, the club cannot use more resources than it has.

We can describe the number of packs using two variables, \(x\) and \(y\). A restriction such as \(x+y\leq 8\) says the total number of packs cannot exceed eight. The restrictions \(x\geq 0\) and \(y\geq 0\) say we cannot produce negative numbers of packs.

Such inequalities are called constraints. A choice satisfying all constraints is called feasible. Our job is to describe all feasible choices before asking which one is best.

Building on Week 7. An objective such as profit tells us what we prefer. Constraints tell us what we are capable of doing. Optimization requires both.

2. Convexity: when the straight path stays inside

A region is convex when the straight line segment joining any two points in the region lies entirely within the region. A filled rectangle is convex. A crescent-shaped region with a gap generally is not.

Why is this useful? The solutions of a system of linear inequalities in two variables form a convex region (when a solution exists). The geometry is especially well-behaved: straight boundary lines enclose a collection of possible decisions.

The word “convex” describes a shape, not whether a choice is good or bad. The interesting part is that geometric structure can simplify optimization.

Try it. If two feasible recipes can both be prepared, and each resource use is linear, why might a halfway mixture of the two recipes also be feasible?
CheckLinear resource totals for a halfway mixture are halfway between the corresponding totals. They remain within the same linear upper and lower bounds.

3. Linear inequalities and half-planes

An equation such as \(2x+3y=12\) describes a line. Replacing the equals sign with an inequality, for example \(2x+3y\leq12\), describes one side of that line along with the boundary.

To graph the inequality, first draw the boundary \(2x+3y=12\). Its intercepts are \((6,0)\) and \((0,4)\). Next, test a point not on the line. At the origin \((0,0)\), \(2(0)+3(0)=0\leq12\), so the side containing the origin satisfies the inequality.

Worked interpretation. If \(x\) uses two hours of labor and \(y\) uses three, \(2x+3y\leq12\) expresses a twelve-hour labor limit. The point \((3,2)\) uses \(2(3)+3(2)=12\) hours, exactly meeting the limit. The point \((4,2)\) uses 14 hours and is not feasible under this constraint.
Try it. Is \((2,2)\) permitted by \(2x+3y\leq12\)?
CheckYes: \(2(2)+3(2)=10\leq12\).

4. The feasible region is the intersection

A real decision often has several restrictions at once. The feasible region is the set of points satisfying all the inequalities simultaneously. Graphically, we look for where the permitted half-planes overlap.

Worked example. Suppose the club must satisfy \(x+y\leq8\), \(2x+y\leq12\), \(x\geq0\), and \(y\geq0\). The first constraint limits the total number of packs. The second limits a resource used twice as fast by packs of type \(x\). The final two constraints keep us in the first quadrant.

The corner points of this feasible region are \((0,0), (6,0), (4,4), (0,8)\). You can check each point by substituting it into the inequalities. For example, \((4,4)\) satisfies \(4+4=8\) and \(2(4)+4=12\).

The point \((6,4)\) violates both resource constraints; even if its profit looks attractive, it cannot be selected in the model.

5. The corner-point principle

For a linear objective on a nonempty bounded polygonal feasible region, a maximum and minimum can be found at corner points. This is the basis of the corner-point method taught in the original PDF chapters.

Suppose each type-\(x\) pack earns $3 in profit and each type-\(y\) pack earns $2. The objective is

\[P=3x+2y.\]

Worked example. Evaluate profit at the corners:
CornerProfit calculationProfit
(0,0)3(0)+2(0)$0
(6,0)3(6)+2(0)$18
(4,4)3(4)+2(4)$20
(0,8)3(0)+2(8)$16

The largest value is $20 at \((4,4)\), so the club should make four packs of each type if the goal is to maximize this modeled profit.

Try it. If the club instead wants to make as many type-\(y\) packs as possible, which corner would it choose?
Check\((0,8)\), because \(y=8\) is the largest feasible value of \(y\) among the corners.

6. Linear programming: maximize or minimize

A linear program combines decision variables, a linear objective, and linear constraints. Some problems ask us to maximize something desirable, such as profit. Others ask us to minimize something costly, such as shipping expense.

For minimization, the constraints may be lower bounds, such as “provide at least six units of service.” The feasible region may extend without bound, but an optimal corner can still exist under suitable conditions.

Worked minimization example. Choose nonnegative \(x,y\) satisfying \(x+y\geq6\) and \(2x+y\geq8\). Minimize \(C=4x+3y\). The relevant boundary corners are \((0,8)\), \((2,4)\), and \((6,0)\). Their costs are $24, $20, and $24, respectively. Thus \((2,4)\) minimizes the cost in this model.

To model a real situation well, always state what the decision variables represent, give units, and explain where the constraints came from. If quantities must be whole numbers, a fractional answer may need additional consideration beyond basic linear programming.

7. Reading a geometric answer responsibly

A beautiful feasible polygon does not tell us whether our assumptions were correct. Resource limits can change, profits can vary, and some consequences may not have been included in the objective.

It is useful to ask what happens if a constraint tightens. The region of possible choices becomes smaller or stays the same. What happens if a resource limit increases? Additional choices may become feasible. The mathematics gives us a way to study these tradeoffs systematically.

Connection to Week 9. Choices also occur in connected systems. A supply may have enough total capacity but still be blocked by a missing connection. Next, we will use graphs and networks to model reachability, pathways, and bottlenecks.
