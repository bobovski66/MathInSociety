Normal Distributions, Z-Scores, and Inference

1. The normal distribution: shape, center, and spread

Many collections of measurements form an approximately bell-shaped pattern: most observations are near the middle, while progressively fewer are far above or below it. A normal distribution is a mathematical model of this symmetric shape.

Its mean \(\mu\) gives the center. Its standard deviation \(\sigma\) controls the spread. A larger standard deviation produces a wider bell; a smaller one produces a narrower bell. We often write \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) to describe a normally distributed variable.

Not every real dataset is normal. Before using the model, consider whether the observed shape reasonably resembles a bell curve.

2. The Empirical Rule makes the bell curve useful

The Empirical Rule gives a memorable approximation: about \(68\%\) of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, about \(95\%\) within two, and about \(99.7\%\) within three.

Worked example. Apple weights have an approximately normal distribution with mean \(180\) grams and standard deviation \(15\) grams. About \(68\%\) weigh between \(165\) and \(195\) grams. About \(95\%\) weigh between \(150\) and \(210\) grams. About \(99.7\%\) weigh between \(135\) and \(225\) grams.

Notice that each interval is centered at 180. Moving farther from the center includes more of the population.

Try it. If a normal distribution has mean \(50\) and standard deviation \(6\), what interval contains approximately \(95\%\) of its values?
CheckTwo standard deviations is \(12\), so the interval is \(50-12=38\) to \(50+12=62\).

3. Z-scores describe a measurement's location

When two measurements use different units or scales, we can describe their positions relative to their own distributions. A z-score is the number of standard deviations an observation lies from the mean:

\[z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}.\]

A positive z-score indicates a value above the mean; a negative one indicates a value below it. A z-score of zero is exactly at the mean.

Worked example. A commuter takes \(59\) minutes to travel to work. In an approximately normal distribution with mean \(35\) minutes and standard deviation \(8\) minutes, \(z=(59-35)/8=3\). The commute is three standard deviations above the mean, so it is unusually long in this model.
Try it. A measurement is \(44\) in a distribution with mean \(50\) and standard deviation \(6\). Find its z-score.
Check\((44-50)/6=-1\).

4. A sample is not the entire population

Suppose you want to know the average number of hours students at a college work each week. Asking every student might be impractical. Instead, you collect a sample and calculate its mean.

The mean of the whole population is a population parameter. A mean calculated from your sample is a sample statistic. We use sample statistics to estimate unknown population parameters.

Two random samples from the same population will usually give slightly different sample means. That does not necessarily mean anyone made a mistake. It is called sampling variability.

Important connection. The spread of individual observations and the uncertainty of a sample mean are not the same quantity. Averaging many independent observations generally produces a more stable estimate than relying on one observation.

5. Confidence intervals: estimating with a margin

A confidence interval reports an estimate together with a reasonable range of uncertainty. Instead of saying the population mean must be exactly 72, we might estimate that it lies somewhere between 70 and 74.

For a sufficiently large representative independent sample, a familiar approximate \(95\%\) interval for the population mean is

\[\bar x \ \pm\ 2\frac{s}{\sqrt n}.\]

The quantity \(s/\sqrt n\) is an estimated standard error of the sample mean. This is a useful approximation, not a promise that any particular interval contains the true mean. More precise procedures, especially for small samples, can use a t-distribution.

Worked example. A random sample of 100 observations has sample mean 72 and sample standard deviation 10. The estimated standard error is \(10/\sqrt{100}=1\). An approximate \(95\%\) confidence interval is \(72\pm2\), or from \(70\) to \(74\).

How to interpret 95%: If the same valid sampling and interval-building procedure were repeated many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the fixed population mean. The confidence level describes the method, not the probability assigned to a particular fixed mean after observing the data.

6. The observer and the estimate

Numbers do not speak entirely for themselves. A sample may be too small, poorly selected, or collected with an unreliable instrument. An attractive formula cannot repair a biased sampling process.

When you read a statistical claim, ask: Who was measured? How were they selected? What is the sample size? How much variation is present? What assumptions were made?

Bringing the ideas together. A thermometer reports 74°F in a greenhouse whose target is 70°F. The difference is 4°F, but before changing the heating or cooling equipment, we might ask how reliable the sensor is, whether readings fluctuate, and whether other measurements agree.
Connection to Week 5. Statistics helps us decide what the observations support. Cybernetics studies what happens after we use those observations to choose an action—and then measure the consequences.
